# Demand Generation

Demand generation is the practice of creating predictable, qualified pipeline. It is not the same as lead generation (which is just collecting names) or brand marketing (which builds long-term equity). Demand gen produces revenue this quarter.

## The Demand Gen Funnel

Most companies still model the funnel as MQL → SQL → Opportunity → Closed Won. The numbers and stages vary, but the structure is similar:

```
Visitors / Reach
  → Leads (filled a form, attended an event)
    → MQLs (Marketing-Qualified Leads — fit + intent signals)
      → SQLs (Sales-Qualified Leads — sales accepted)
        → Opportunities (real evaluation underway)
          → Closed Won
```

**Conversion benchmarks** (B2B SaaS, mid-funnel):
- MQL → SQL: 40-60% in well-run programs; below 30% means MQL definition is too loose
- SQL → Opportunity: 50-70%
- Opportunity → Closed Won: 20-30% for mid-market; 10-20% for enterprise

**Two truths about funnel metrics:**
1. The bottom-up math matters more than absolute numbers. To hit $10M ARR with $50k ACV, you need ~200 closed won. At 20% close rate: 1,000 opportunities. At 60% SQL→Opp: 1,667 SQLs. At 50% MQL→SQL: 3,333 MQLs. Plan backwards.
2. The funnel is a model, not reality. Real buyers move backward, skip stages, evaluate in parallel. Use the funnel for forecasting, not as a literal customer journey.

## The Two Modes — Capture vs. Create

**Demand Capture** — people already looking; you make sure they find you
- Channels: SEO (especially bottom-of-funnel terms), SEM/Google Ads, comparison-site listings (G2, Capterra), review-driven SEO
- Strengths: high intent, faster conversion, lower CAC per lead
- Limits: bounded by existing search volume; competitive bidding compresses margins

**Demand Creation** — people not actively looking; you create the need
- Channels: content marketing, social, podcasts, events, paid social, outbound (when targeted to right ICP)
- Strengths: scalable, drives long-term category leadership, builds brand
- Limits: longer payback, harder to attribute, easy to confuse activity with results

**The mix:**
- Pre-PMF: 80% demand capture (proves intent, fast learning)
- Growth phase: 50/50 (capture pays for itself, creation builds future pipeline)
- Mature category: 30% capture, 70% creation (capture market is mostly served)

Companies that over-index on capture stall at the size of existing demand. Companies that over-index on creation burn cash with no near-term pipeline.

## Channel Economics — How to Read Them

Track each channel independently. Blended metrics hide reality.

**Per-channel metrics:**
- *CPL (cost per lead)* — how much you spent / how many leads
- *Lead-to-customer conversion rate* — leads from this channel that became customers
- *CAC by channel* — fully-loaded cost / customers acquired
- *Payback period by channel* — CAC / monthly gross margin per customer
- *LTV/CAC by channel* — same denominator, lifetime value of cohort from this channel

**Channel saturation:** every channel has a ceiling. You spend $10k, get 100 leads at $100 CPL. Spend $50k, you might get 350 leads at $143 CPL. Spend $200k, you might get 800 leads at $250 CPL. Track CPL as you scale spend; the breaking point tells you the channel ceiling.

**Channel decay:** ad platforms, SEO, and outbound all have natural decay. Algorithms change, content gets stale, audiences saturate. Plan for 20-30% efficiency decay per year if you don't actively reinvest.

## Account-Based Marketing (ABM)

For enterprise GTM, ABM has largely replaced traditional inbound. Core idea: instead of casting a wide net, identify the 50-500 accounts most likely to buy, and engage them as named accounts.

**Tiering:**
- Tier 1: top 25-50 named accounts, 1:1 personalization, exec-level engagement
- Tier 2: 100-250 accounts, 1:few programs (industry verticals)
- Tier 3: 250-2000 accounts, 1:many programmatic ABM (firmographic targeting)

**Plays:**
- Industry events / dinners for Tier 1 (executive engagement)
- Custom content (industry reports, vertical-specific case studies) for Tier 2
- Display retargeting, sponsored content for Tier 3

**Sales-marketing alignment:**
- Shared account list (defined criteria, not arbitrary list)
- Joint outbound (rep + SDR + marketing-supported)
- Shared metrics (pipeline created by account, not by lead source)

ABM done well produces higher win rates and larger ACVs. Done badly (no real personalization, no sales alignment) it's expensive lead generation.

## Outbound — When and How

Outbound is not dead, but spray-and-pray is. Outbound that works in 2026:

- **Triggered**: outreach prompted by a real event (funding round, exec hire, product launch, hiring spree). 3-5x response rate of cold.
- **Targeted**: tight ICP, fewer than 50 accounts per rep per week
- **Personalized**: research-led, specific reason for the outreach, not "I saw your LinkedIn"
- **Multi-channel**: email + LinkedIn + voicemail in sequence
- **Sequenced**: 6-10 touches over 2-3 weeks; most responses come on touch 4-7

Outbound metrics to watch:
- Reply rate (>5% is good for cold; >20% for triggered/warm)
- Positive reply rate (interested, not "unsubscribe") — 1-3% is realistic
- Meetings booked per 100 contacts
- Meetings → opportunities (qualifies your targeting)

When outbound numbers are below benchmark, the diagnosis is usually targeting (wrong ICP) before it's messaging.

## Content Marketing

Content is the long game. 6-18 month payback. Compounds over time.

**Content by funnel stage:**
- **Top-of-funnel** (awareness): industry reports, original research, thought leadership essays, podcasts
- **Middle-of-funnel** (consideration): how-to guides, framework articles, comparison content, webinars
- **Bottom-of-funnel** (decision): case studies, ROI calculators, demos, comparison guides ("X vs. Y")

**The 80/20 of B2B content:**
- 80% of value comes from 20% of pieces
- The 20% is usually: original research, definitive guides on bottom-funnel terms, founder essays on category-defining ideas
- Stop publishing weekly newsletters that nobody reads. Invest the time in pieces that move the needle.

**SEO discipline:**
- Bottom-of-funnel first (product/solution/category terms with buyer intent)
- Then middle-funnel (problem-aware terms)
- Top-of-funnel only after the others are paying
- Track ranking + organic traffic + conversion, not just traffic

## Attribution — The Truth

Marketing attribution is hard. Anyone who tells you they have it solved is selling something.

**The best practical approach:**
- Multi-touch attribution as the model (first touch, last touch, even-weight, time-decay)
- Self-reported attribution as the truth check ("how did you hear about us?" on the form)
- Marketing-influenced pipeline as the broad metric (any touch in the journey, regardless of weight)
- Marketing-sourced pipeline as the strict metric (first touch was a marketing channel)

**Use directionally, not surgically:**
- "This channel is contributing to growth" — defensible
- "This channel contributed 17.3% of revenue" — false precision

## Common Demand Gen Failures

1. **Optimizing for leads instead of pipeline** — MQLs that don't convert just look busy
2. **Throwing money at paid before product-market fit** — accelerates the proof that the product isn't ready
3. **Conflating brand and demand gen** — brand-only programs that never deliver pipeline; conversely, demand gen with no brand investment plateaus
4. **No sales-marketing alignment on what an MQL is** — sales rejects half of marketing's MQLs, marketing thinks sales is lazy, repeat
5. **Stopping a content investment too early** — content needs 12-18 months. Stopping at month 6 means writing off the investment before the payoff curve

## Demand Gen Diagnostic

Every quarter, answer:
1. What was our pipeline coverage (pipeline value vs. quota)?
2. What was net-new pipeline created, by source?
3. Which source has the best CAC payback? Which has the worst?
4. What is the trend in MQL→SQL conversion? (If declining, MQL quality is degrading)
5. Where in the funnel is the biggest drop-off, and what experiments are we running there?

If you can't answer these, demand gen is "marketing activity" — not a managed system.
