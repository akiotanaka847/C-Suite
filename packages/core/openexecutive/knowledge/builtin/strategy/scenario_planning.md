# Scenario Planning

Most strategic plans assume a future that looks like the present, extrapolated. Scenario planning is the discipline of considering futures that look different — and pre-deciding what you'd do in each one.

## When Scenario Planning Is Worth It

Not every decision needs scenarios. Most don't. Use scenario planning when:

- The decision is large, irreversible, or has multi-year consequences (fundraise size, market entry, M&A, headcount commitments)
- The environment has high uncertainty on factors you don't control (macro, regulation, competitive moves, technology shifts)
- The cost of being wrong is asymmetric — one bad outcome is much worse than the other side is good

Skip scenario planning when: you're optimizing within a stable environment, the decision is reversible quickly, or you're under such acute pressure that any plan beats no plan.

## The Four-Quadrant Method

The most-used scenario method. Pick two independent uncertainties that matter most; plot them as axes; develop a coherent story for each quadrant.

**Process:**
1. Identify the decision in scope (e.g., "how large to size Series B")
2. List driving forces (market growth, customer behavior, capital availability, regulation, competitive moves)
3. Cluster into 5-10 categories
4. Score each on impact (high/low) and uncertainty (high/low)
5. Pick the two highest impact + highest uncertainty as axes
6. Plot four quadrants, name each, develop the story

**Example for a SaaS company:**
- *Axis 1*: B2B IT budgets (expanding vs. compressing)
- *Axis 2*: AI displacement of category (fast vs. slow)
- *Scenarios*: "Growth Continues" (expand budgets, slow AI), "AI Boom" (expand budgets, fast AI), "Conserve and Wait" (compress budgets, slow AI), "Existential Squeeze" (compress budgets, fast AI)

For each scenario:
- What does the world look like in 18 months?
- What does our business look like?
- What's the right move now?

**The decision filter:**
- Which decisions are robust across all four scenarios? (Do those.)
- Which decisions only work in one or two? (Hedge.)
- Which decisions are bets on a specific scenario? (Take if conviction is high; otherwise wait.)

## War-Gaming (Adversarial Scenarios)

A variant where you specifically model competitive responses. Used when the dominant uncertainty is competitor behavior.

**Process:**
1. Assign teams to play each major competitor and one to play the market
2. Each team gets a brief on their player's incentives and constraints
3. Play moves over 2-4 rounds simulating 12-24 months
4. Document the moves and counter-moves

**What it reveals:**
- Competitive responses you weren't planning for
- Defenses you'd need against attacks that haven't happened yet
- Strategic moves that look great in isolation but invite obvious retaliation

Most valuable for: pricing wars, M&A defense, new market entry against entrenched players.

## Pre-mortems

A retrospective on a future failure. Imagine the project has failed 12 months from now; ask the team to write the autopsy.

**Why it works:**
- People are better at imagining what could go wrong than predicting probabilities directly
- Surfaces risks that "everything is fine" reviews suppress
- Builds shared understanding of failure modes before launch

**Process (60-90 min):**
1. Frame: "It's a year from now. This project has failed. Why?"
2. Each person writes their own list (5-10 reasons), independently — no group discussion yet
3. Combine the lists, dedupe, theme
4. For each top failure mode: how likely, how would we detect, what's the mitigation
5. Identify the 2-3 risks that warrant ongoing monitoring

Pre-mortems take 90 minutes and routinely catch issues that would have surfaced at month 6.

## Real Options Thinking

A way to value flexibility under uncertainty. Comes from finance but applies broadly.

**The core idea:**
- A decision that locks you into one path has no "option value"
- A decision that preserves multiple paths has option value, even if it's more expensive upfront
- Under high uncertainty, paying for optionality is often the right call

**Real options examples:**
- Lease vs. buy real estate — leasing preserves the option to exit
- Build vs. buy software — building preserves the option to customize; buying preserves the option to switch vendors
- Multi-year contract with auto-renewal vs. annual contract — annual preserves the option to negotiate
- Hire a contractor vs. hire an employee — contractor preserves the option to scale down

**The discipline:**
- When uncertainty is high, value optionality
- When uncertainty is low, commit and capture the cost savings of commitment
- Re-evaluate as uncertainty resolves

## Decision Trees

A simpler visualization for sequential decisions under uncertainty.

**Structure:**
- Decision nodes (squares): choices you make
- Chance nodes (circles): events you don't control, with probabilities
- Endpoint nodes (triangles): outcomes with values

Walk through the tree assigning probabilities and values, then calculate expected values to find the optimal path.

**When useful:**
- Sequential decisions where later decisions depend on earlier outcomes
- M&A bid strategies
- Litigation settlement decisions
- Launch sequencing decisions

**When NOT useful:**
- When probabilities are essentially unknowable (most strategic decisions)
- When the value of outcomes is highly subjective
- When the tree gets so complex it's no longer interpretable

The tree's main value is forcing explicit assumptions about probabilities and values — not the numerical answer at the end.

## Common Scenario Planning Failures

**1. Forecasting disguised as scenarios** — running three forecasts that are all variants of the base case (optimistic / base / pessimistic). Not actual scenarios; just sensitivity analysis. Useful but different.

**2. Too many scenarios** — six or seven distinct futures. Cognitive overload; no decision discipline.

**3. Scenarios that don't change decisions** — if your action plan is the same in every scenario, why did you do the scenario planning?

**4. Treating scenarios as predictions** — picking a "most likely" scenario and planning to it. Misses the point. Scenarios are stress-tests of decisions, not forecasts.

**5. One-and-done** — running scenario planning once at the offsite and never updating. Scenarios should be revisited quarterly as uncertainty resolves and new uncertainty emerges.

## The Output of Good Scenario Planning

A scenario-planning exercise should produce three deliverables:

1. **A short list of monitored signals** — leading indicators that tell you which scenario is actually unfolding
2. **A set of pre-committed actions** — "if signal X crosses threshold Y, we'll do Z" — so you don't have to re-decide in the moment
3. **A list of robust decisions** — choices that look good across all scenarios, made with conviction now

If the output is just "we discussed it," the work didn't land.

## The Diagnostic

Before committing to a major decision, ask:
1. What are the 2-3 biggest uncertainties that could change the right answer?
2. What does the world look like if each uncertainty resolves badly?
3. Is our plan robust to those outcomes, or does it require the favorable case?
4. What's the leading signal that would tell us we're in the bad case?
5. What would we do if we saw that signal next quarter?

If you can't answer all five, you're betting on the future you hope for, not the future you're prepared for.
