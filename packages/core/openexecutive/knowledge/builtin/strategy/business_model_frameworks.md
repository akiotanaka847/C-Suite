# Business Model Frameworks

A business model is how a company creates, delivers, and captures value. Most strategy failures are business model failures dressed up as execution failures. Get the model right first.

## Business Model Canvas (Osterwalder)

Nine building blocks on a single page. Use it to stress-test a new business or audit an existing one.

**Right side — value delivery to customers:**
1. **Customer Segments** — for whom are we creating value? Mass market, niche, segmented, diversified, multi-sided platform.
2. **Value Propositions** — what bundle of products/services creates value for each segment? Newness, performance, customization, design, brand, price, cost reduction, risk reduction, accessibility, convenience.
3. **Channels** — how do we reach customers? Awareness → evaluation → purchase → delivery → after-sales.
4. **Customer Relationships** — personal assistance, self-service, automated services, communities, co-creation.
5. **Revenue Streams** — asset sale, usage fee, subscription, lending/renting, licensing, brokerage, advertising.

**Left side — the cost of delivering it:**
6. **Key Resources** — physical, intellectual, human, financial.
7. **Key Activities** — production, problem-solving, platform/network.
8. **Key Partnerships** — strategic alliances, coopetition, joint ventures, buyer-supplier.
9. **Cost Structure** — cost-driven vs. value-driven; fixed vs. variable; economies of scale vs. scope.

Test of a good BMC: a stranger can read it and explain how the business makes money in 90 seconds.

## Lean Canvas (Maurya — for early-stage)

A BMC variant tuned for startups. Replaces four blocks:

- Customer Segments → **Problem** (top 3 problems for the segment)
- Customer Relationships → **Solution** (top 3 features that address the problems)
- Key Activities → **Key Metrics** (the 1-2 numbers that prove traction)
- Key Resources → **Unfair Advantage** (something competitors can't easily copy or buy)

Use Lean Canvas pre-product-market fit, BMC post-PMF.

## Value Proposition Canvas

Zoom into one customer segment and one value proposition. Two halves:

**Customer profile:**
- **Jobs** — functional, social, emotional jobs the customer is trying to get done
- **Pains** — undesired outcomes, obstacles, risks
- **Gains** — required, expected, desired, unexpected outcomes

**Value map:**
- **Products & services** — what you offer
- **Pain relievers** — how you specifically eliminate pains
- **Gain creators** — how you specifically produce gains

**Fit test:** can you draw a line from each pain to a specific pain reliever, and each gain to a gain creator? If not, the value proposition is aspirational, not real.

## Wardley Mapping (for technology-heavy businesses)

A map of your value chain plotted against evolution: Genesis → Custom-built → Product → Commodity/Utility.

Why it matters: components evolve. What was a competitive moat (custom-built infrastructure) becomes a commodity (cloud). Treating utilities like differentiators is how companies sink money into things customers no longer pay for. Treating differentiators like utilities is how companies miss the next moat.

Process:
1. Anchor: the user need at the top
2. Decompose the value chain that satisfies it
3. Place each component on the evolution axis based on how mature/commoditized it is
4. Identify climatic patterns (this component will inevitably commoditize) and play your strategy around them

## Platform vs. Linear Business Models

A linear business turns inputs into outputs and sells them. A platform business orchestrates interactions between two or more sides of a market.

Linear levers: cost reduction, vertical integration, brand, scale.

Platform levers: network effects (each additional user makes the platform more valuable to others), data effects, multi-sided pricing (subsidize the side that brings the other side).

Key platform questions:
- Which side is harder to attract? Subsidize that side.
- What is the chicken-and-egg solution? Single-side first, seed the harder side, or one product first then open up to a platform.
- What governance prevents the platform from being abused (spam, fraud, race to the bottom)?

Most companies that call themselves platforms are linear businesses with an API. A real platform requires that the value users derive comes primarily from other users, not from you.

## Choosing a Model — A Forcing Question

If your customers stopped using your product tomorrow, who would they replace you with? If the answer is "no one — they'd just go without," your value proposition is weak. If the answer is "five obvious competitors," your moat is weak. If the answer is "they couldn't replace us without rebuilding three things at high cost," you have a defensible model.
