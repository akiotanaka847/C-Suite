# OKR Design and Execution

## What OKRs Are (and Are Not)

OKRs (Objectives and Key Results) are a goal-setting framework that aligns organization-wide priorities and creates transparency about what matters. They are not:
- A performance review tool (don't tie OKRs to compensation)
- A task list (KRs measure outcomes, not outputs)
- A substitute for strategy (OKRs operationalize strategy, they don't replace it)

## Writing Good Objectives

An Objective answers: "Where are we going this quarter/year?"

Criteria:
- Qualitative and inspirational, not a number
- Achievable in the time period
- Unambiguous — the team should agree on whether it was achieved
- Directionally correct with company strategy

Good: "Establish Open Executive as the default AI executive tool for Series A-B companies"
Bad: "Grow the business" (too vague) or "Launch 5 features" (output, not outcome)

## Writing Good Key Results

A Key Result answers: "How will we know we got there?"

Criteria:
- Measurable with a specific number and deadline
- Outcome-based, not task-based
- 3-5 per Objective
- Should require real effort to hit — 70% achievement is the target, not 100%

Good KR: "Net Revenue Retention reaches 115% by Q4"
Bad KR: "Implement customer success process" (task, not outcome)

## OKR Grading

Score 0.0 to 1.0:
- 1.0 = fully achieved (should be rare — if always 1.0, you're setting sandbagged OKRs)
- 0.7 = success (stretch goal system; this is the target)
- <0.4 = failure (requires retrospective on what went wrong)

Rule: if your team consistently scores 1.0 on all OKRs, make them harder. If they consistently score <0.4, either the goals are unrealistic or execution is broken — diagnose which.

## OKR Cadence

**Annual OKRs**: Set company-level direction. CEO owns these, board sees them.

**Quarterly OKRs**: Operationalize annual OKRs into 90-day sprints. Team-level OKRs cascade from these.

**Weekly/biweekly check-ins**: 10-minute OKR review: what's on track, what's at risk, what needs help. Not a status update — a flagging mechanism.

## Common OKR Mistakes

1. **Too many OKRs**: Companies should have 3-5 company OKRs. Teams should have 2-3. More than that means no real priorities.

2. **KRs that are tasks**: "Launch feature X" is a task. "Feature X drives 20% of new signups by Q3" is a KR.

3. **OKRs don't connect**: Team OKRs should visibly ladder up to company OKRs. If they don't, the team is optimizing for something disconnected from company goals.

4. **Set and forget**: OKRs reviewed only at end of quarter are decoration. Weekly check-ins are mandatory.

5. **Sandbagging**: If every team hits 100% of OKRs every quarter, the OKR process has been captured to protect bonuses. Fix the incentive structure.

## Alignment Process

1. CEO + leadership team set company OKRs (top-down direction)
2. Teams draft their own OKRs (bottom-up)
3. Alignment conversation: do team OKRs actually support company OKRs?
4. Publish all OKRs company-wide — transparency is the point
5. No more than 2 layers of OKR hierarchy for companies under 200 people
