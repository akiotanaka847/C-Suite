# Competitive Analysis Frameworks

## Porter's Five Forces

Use to assess the structural attractiveness of an industry and a company's competitive position within it.

**1. Threat of New Entrants**
- Barriers to entry: capital requirements, economies of scale, switching costs, brand loyalty, regulatory barriers, proprietary technology
- High barriers = lower threat = more attractive industry
- Ask: what would it cost a well-funded competitor to build what we have in 18 months?

**2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers**
- High when: few suppliers, differentiated inputs, high switching costs, credible forward integration threat
- Mitigation: diversify suppliers, develop alternatives, build supplier relationships, vertical integration

**3. Bargaining Power of Buyers**
- High when: few large buyers, undifferentiated product, low switching costs, credible backward integration threat
- Mitigation: increase switching costs, differentiate, expand buyer base, build brand loyalty

**4. Threat of Substitutes**
- Not just direct competitors — alternative ways buyers can meet the same need
- Example: Zoom substituting for business travel, not just other video conferencing tools

**5. Competitive Rivalry**
- High when: many similar competitors, slow growth, high fixed costs, low differentiation, high exit barriers
- Intense rivalry compresses margins across all players

**Applying the Framework**
Rate each force (low/medium/high). Industries with all-low forces (rare) are very profitable. Most companies compete in industries with mixed profiles — identify the 1-2 forces that actually drive your margin pressure.

## Competitive Positioning Matrix

Plot competitors on two dimensions that your target customers actually care about. Common pairs:
- Price vs. Quality/Features
- Specialization vs. Breadth
- Speed vs. Reliability
- Enterprise vs. SMB focus

The best positions are either (1) leader on both desired dimensions, or (2) clear leader on the dimension your target customer values most.

## Jobs-to-be-Done Competitive Analysis

Customers "hire" products to do a job. Your real competitors are all the ways customers can get that job done — including doing nothing.

Process:
1. Define the job precisely: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]"
2. Map all current solutions customers use (including workarounds, manual processes)
3. Assess each solution on: reliability, speed, cost, social acceptability, side effects
4. Find the underserved job or segment where current solutions fall short

## Win/Loss Analysis

Run structured interviews with recent won and lost deals:
- Why did they start looking? (trigger event)
- What alternatives did they evaluate?
- What tipped the decision?
- What would make them switch back?

Pattern: look for 3+ losses citing the same reason — that is a signal, not noise.

## Competitive Intelligence Sources

- Job postings (reveals investment priorities)
- LinkedIn headcount changes (growth/contraction signals)
- G2/Capterra/Trustpilot reviews (customer complaints = your opportunity)
- App store reviews
- GitHub (open source activity)
- Patent filings (technology direction)
- Press releases and blog posts (strategic priorities they're announcing)
- Talk to their churned customers — most will speak openly
