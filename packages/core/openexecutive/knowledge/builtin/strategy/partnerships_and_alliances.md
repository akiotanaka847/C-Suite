# Partnerships and Alliances

Partnerships are the most under-invested and over-talked-about lever in most companies. Done well, they extend reach, plug capability gaps, and create defensive moats. Done badly, they consume executive time, create complex contracts, and produce nothing.

## The Partnership Types

Different partnership types serve different purposes. Confusing them is the most common failure.

### Technology integrations

Your product integrates with another product (one-way or mutual). Salesforce + Marketo, Slack + Zoom, Stripe + Shopify.

- *Goal*: increase product value via interoperability; reduce switching cost for buyers; tap partner's customer base for distribution
- *Investment*: engineering time; usually no revenue share
- *Success metric*: # of mutual customers; integration usage rate

### Co-selling partnerships

Two sales teams work together to sell to the same customer (often a complementary product).

- *Goal*: larger deal sizes; faster sales cycles; warmer introductions
- *Investment*: account team time; joint pipeline management; partner-enablement materials
- *Success metric*: partner-influenced ARR; deals closed jointly

### Channel / reseller partnerships

A third party sells your product on your behalf, typically with a margin (20-40%).

- *Goal*: distribution reach you can't build in-house; geographic or vertical expertise
- *Investment*: partner economics; enablement; deal registration system
- *Success metric*: % of revenue from channel; channel productivity (revenue per active partner)

### Strategic alliances

A deeper relationship — joint product development, shared GTM, sometimes equity stakes.

- *Goal*: combined capability neither party has alone
- *Investment*: significant exec time; legal complexity; cultural and process integration
- *Success metric*: joint outcomes (revenue, market share); often hard to attribute cleanly

### Embedded / OEM partnerships

Your product is embedded inside another product (white-labeled or co-branded).

- *Goal*: distribution at scale; revenue with low CAC
- *Investment*: deep technical integration; long sales cycles; partner has significant leverage
- *Success metric*: revenue per partner; partner retention

## The Make / Buy / Partner Decision

Before pursuing any partnership, force the question through this filter:

- **Make** when: the capability is core to differentiation; you have the talent; time-to-market isn't fatal
- **Buy** when: the capability is core but not yet built; the gap is >12 months; the target has unique IP
- **Partner** when: the capability is adjacent (not core); the partner has clearer comparative advantage; partner economics are workable

Default bias: Make > Partner > Buy. Partnerships look like free leverage; they're not.

## Why Most Partnerships Fail

**1. No clear customer outcome**
- Partnership announced for the press release, no customer use case
- 12 months later: zero joint pipeline, partnership quietly dissolves
- *Fix*: define the customer outcome FIRST. If you can't, don't do the partnership.

**2. Asymmetric investment**
- One side committed, one side casual
- The committed side does all the work, expects partner reciprocity, gets disappointed
- *Fix*: tier partners by commitment level; concentrate investment on Tier 1

**3. Wrong owner**
- BD owns the partnership; product/sales/CS don't know about it
- Product changes break the integration; sales doesn't position partner; CS doesn't refer
- *Fix*: partnerships need an owner in each affected function, not just BD

**4. Misaligned incentives**
- Sales rep compensation doesn't credit partner-influenced deals
- Reps don't refer to partner; partnership withers
- *Fix*: explicit comp credit for partner-driven and partner-influenced deals

**5. Channel conflict**
- Direct sales team and channel partner both pursue the same account
- Customer plays them off each other; both sides lose
- *Fix*: clear rules of engagement; deal registration system; named accounts for direct vs. channel

## Partnership Economics

For any partnership, model the unit economics before signing:

**Revenue side:**
- Expected ARR contribution per quarter (be ruthless; partner pipelines materialize at 25-50% of projection)
- Margin to the partner (revenue share, referral fee, integration premium)
- Net revenue per partner-driven deal

**Cost side:**
- Engineering for integration (initial + ongoing maintenance)
- BD and partner ops headcount
- Enablement materials and time
- Marketing co-investment
- Legal time on the agreement

**Payback question:**
- How many partner-sourced deals to recover the investment?
- Does the partner relationship pay back within 12 months at realistic volumes?

If the math requires "this could be huge" optimism rather than concrete sourcing volume, you have a wish, not a partnership.

## Partner Tiering

Most companies treat all partners equally and dilute investment. The right approach is explicit tiering.

**Tier 1 — Strategic (5-10 partners)**
- Significant joint outcomes; named exec sponsors; quarterly business reviews
- Highest investment: dedicated BD, joint roadmap conversations, executive engagement
- Expected: 60-70% of partner-driven revenue

**Tier 2 — Active (20-50 partners)**
- Real pipeline contribution; standard enablement; monthly check-ins
- Moderate investment: partner manager owns multiple
- Expected: 20-30% of partner-driven revenue

**Tier 3 — Long tail (100+ partners)**
- Self-service relationship; portal, documentation, certifications
- Low marginal investment per partner
- Expected: 5-15% of partner-driven revenue

The tier structure forces explicit choice: where do we invest, where do we automate, where do we walk away.

## The Partnership Agreement

Common structure for a meaningful partnership:

**Commercial terms:**
- What's exchanged (referrals, joint sales, technical integration, co-marketing)
- Revenue share or referral fees (with clear definitions and timing)
- Territory or vertical exclusivity (if any — usually a bad idea)
- Term and termination (typically 1-2 year initial term, mutual termination right)

**Operational terms:**
- Named contacts on each side
- Cadence of meetings and reviews
- Shared metrics and reporting
- Joint pipeline visibility

**IP and confidentiality:**
- Each party retains their IP
- Mutual NDA covering shared roadmap, customer info
- Trademark usage rules

**Customer terms:**
- Who owns the customer relationship
- Who provides support (and to whom)
- Data sharing rules

Avoid: exclusivity for territories without minimum revenue commitments; revenue shares that create perverse incentives; partnership agreements that look like joint ventures without the legal structure of one.

## Co-Marketing Discipline

Co-marketing is the easiest entry point for a new partnership — and the most over-invested area.

**Effective co-marketing:**
- Joint customer case study with specific outcomes
- Co-hosted event or webinar with named target accounts invited
- Integration launch announcement with engineering deep-dive content
- Joint research report or industry survey

**Ineffective co-marketing:**
- Press release announcing the partnership (no customer outcome)
- Co-branded swag
- Re-tweeting each other's content
- A meaningless logo on each website's "partners" page

**The 80/20:** invest in co-marketing that ties to a customer outcome. Pass on co-marketing that's about looking active.

## When to Kill a Partnership

The most under-used partnership skill: knowing when to end one.

**Signals:**
- Less than 5% of expected pipeline contribution after 12 months
- Partner team has reorganized or de-prioritized the relationship
- Joint customers complaining about the integration or handoff
- Your sales team avoids the partner in deals

**The conversation:**
- Be direct ("we haven't seen this work for either of us")
- Offer a graceful wind-down (mutual letter, no recrimination)
- Maintain the relationship — partnerships fail; people remain. Today's failed BD is tomorrow's senior buyer.

## The Partnership Diagnostic

Each quarter, for the top 10 partnerships:
1. What customer outcomes have we produced together this quarter?
2. What's the partner-driven pipeline trend (growing, flat, declining)?
3. Who on our side is responsible? Are they engaged?
4. What's our commitment level vs. theirs?
5. Should we double down, maintain, or wind down?

The discipline is the same as your direct customer relationships: actively managed, regularly evaluated, ruthlessly pruned.
