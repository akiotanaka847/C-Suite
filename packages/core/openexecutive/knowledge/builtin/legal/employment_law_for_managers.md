# Employment Law for Managers

*Note: General educational information, not legal advice. Employment law varies materially by jurisdiction (federal, state, country). Consult employment counsel for specific situations.*

This is not the law manual. It's the manager-level awareness that prevents the situations that require expensive legal intervention. Most lawsuits come from manager actions that ignored basics that could have been avoided.

## At-Will Employment (and Its Limits)

US default: employment is "at-will" — either party can end it at any time, with or without cause, for any lawful reason.

**Important exceptions:**
- *Discrimination*: cannot terminate based on protected class (see below)
- *Retaliation*: cannot terminate for protected activity (reporting harassment, taking FMLA leave, etc.)
- *Implied contract*: handbooks and offer letters can create contractual obligations limiting at-will
- *Public policy*: cannot terminate for refusing to do something illegal, or for exercising legal rights (jury duty, voting)
- *Specific contracts*: union agreements, employment contracts, severance agreements override at-will

**Outside the US:**
- Most countries have "just cause" requirements — termination requires documented performance issues or legitimate business reasons
- Notice periods, severance, and procedural requirements are substantial
- Mass layoffs trigger additional government consultation requirements (works councils in EU)
- DO NOT assume US-style at-will outside the US

## Protected Classes (US Federal)

Cannot make employment decisions (hiring, firing, promotion, comp, training, terms) based on:

- Race
- Color
- Religion
- Sex (including pregnancy, sexual orientation, gender identity)
- National origin
- Age (40+)
- Disability (including perceived disability)
- Genetic information
- Veteran / military status

**State and local laws often add more protections:**
- Marital status, family status, parental status
- Source of income, criminal history (especially under "ban the box" laws)
- Political affiliation
- Hairstyle (under CROWN Act in many jurisdictions)
- And more

**The rule:** assume any characteristic could be protected somewhere; train managers to base decisions on documented job-related criteria only.

## Hiring — Where Most Legal Risk Begins

**Pre-employment do NOT ask:**
- Age, birthdate, year of graduation
- Marital status, family plans, childcare
- Religious affiliations
- Disability status (only relevant if affects ability to do the job with reasonable accommodation)
- Citizenship status before offer (can ask "are you authorized to work in the US" after offer)
- Salary history (illegal in growing list of states/cities)
- Criminal history (timing rules vary by jurisdiction; "ban the box" requires offer before background check)

**Job descriptions:**
- Focus on essential functions and required qualifications
- Avoid language that signals age/gender preference ("recent grad", "rockstar", "young team")
- "Bona fide occupational qualification" (BFOQ) exceptions are narrow

**Interview discipline:**
- Same questions to all candidates (structured interviewing reduces bias and legal risk)
- Document interview scores against job-related criteria
- Train interviewers on what NOT to ask
- Reference checks: ask job-related questions only

**Background checks:**
- Comply with FCRA (US federal) — written consent, copy of report to candidate, opportunity to dispute
- Don't use background check info to discriminate (especially criminal history under EEOC guidance)
- Drug testing rules vary substantially by state (especially with cannabis legalization)

## Compensation — Legal Considerations

**Equal Pay Act + state equal-pay laws:**
- Equal pay for equal work regardless of sex (federal); equal work or substantially similar work (some states)
- Gender pay-gap analyses now legally required in some jurisdictions (UK, California, EU directive coming)
- Document compensation decisions with non-protected-class rationale

**Pay transparency laws:**
- Growing list of states require salary ranges in job postings (NY, CO, CA, WA, others)
- Pay range disclosure on request (some jurisdictions)
- Bands should be defensible — large ranges with no internal calibration draw scrutiny

**Overtime (FLSA):**
- "Exempt" vs. "non-exempt" classification — many companies misclassify
- Exempt requires meeting specific tests (salary level + job duties)
- Misclassification → unpaid overtime liability with multipliers and penalties
- New federal salary threshold for exempt regularly updated; check current rules

**Tip pooling, commissions, bonuses:**
- Each has specific rules; tip pooling is heavily regulated; commissions due even after termination depending on plan language

## Leave Laws

**Federal (US):**
- FMLA: up to 12 weeks unpaid, job-protected leave for serious health condition, family care, military leave (eligibility: 50+ employees, 12 months tenure)
- ADA: reasonable accommodation for disability, which may include extended leave
- USERRA: military leave protections
- Pregnancy Discrimination Act: pregnancy treated as any other temporary disability

**State (US):**
- Paid family leave (CA, NY, NJ, MA, others — growing)
- Paid sick leave (many states + cities)
- Pregnancy accommodation requirements beyond federal
- Bereavement leave (some states)

**International:**
- Most countries have far more generous statutory leave than US
- Parental leave often 4-12+ months in EU, Canada, etc.
- Annual vacation often 4-6 weeks minimum
- Don't apply US-style leave policies to non-US employees

**Operational discipline:**
- Track leave entitlements per employee per jurisdiction
- Don't ask about FMLA before it's requested (creates retaliation evidence)
- Reasonable accommodation conversations are interactive — document them
- Return-to-work conversations must follow procedural requirements

## Harassment and Discrimination

**Prevention:**
- Annual anti-harassment training (mandatory in many states for managers + employees)
- Clear written policy with reporting mechanism
- Multiple reporting channels (not just direct manager — who might be the offender)
- Anti-retaliation policy explicit

**Response when a complaint is raised:**
- Immediate investigation — not "we'll get to it"
- Investigation by trained party (often HR or external) who is not in either party's reporting chain
- Confidentiality to the extent possible (cannot promise complete confidentiality)
- Document interviews, evidence, conclusions
- Outcome communicated to complainant within reasonable time
- Appropriate corrective action if substantiated
- No retaliation against complainant (regardless of investigation outcome)

**Common manager failures:**
- Dismissing complaints as "personality conflicts"
- Investigating the complainant ("why are they bringing this up now?")
- Mediating between complainant and accused (inappropriate for serious complaints)
- Promising confidentiality you can't keep
- Retaliation (even subtle — schedule changes, removal from projects) post-complaint

## Performance Management (Legal Lens)

The single best legal protection in termination cases is a documented, consistent performance management process.

**Per-employee documentation:**
- Goals and expectations (documented and acknowledged)
- Performance reviews (signed by both parties)
- Feedback in 1:1s (written notes preserved)
- Performance issues raised in writing with specific examples
- PIPs documented with measurable improvement criteria

**Why this matters legally:**
- In a termination challenge, the company must show a non-discriminatory reason
- Contemporaneous documentation of performance issues is strong evidence
- Manufactured documentation after the fact is detectable and damaging

**Consistency:**
- Similar issues should be treated similarly across employees
- If one employee got a PIP for performance issue X, others with the same issue should get PIPs too
- Inconsistent treatment is evidence of pretext (discriminatory intent disguised as performance)

## Terminations

**Pre-termination checklist:**
- Performance issues documented?
- PIP process completed (if applicable)?
- Consistent with how similar situations were handled?
- Recent absence/leave/complaint that could create retaliation appearance?
- HR + legal review?
- Severance offered in exchange for release?

**The termination conversation:**
- Short, clear, kind
- Don't re-litigate or over-explain
- Don't share severance with anyone else (confidentiality)
- Don't promise references beyond the company policy
- Don't make off-the-cuff statements about the reason — stick to the prepared, reviewed language

**The release agreement:**
- Standard practice for severance > nominal
- Specific consideration (severance) in exchange for release of claims
- Required revocation period (federal: 7 days for 40+; OWBPA rules)
- Must be in plain English
- Should be reviewed by employee with opportunity for legal review

**WARN Act (US federal mass layoffs):**
- Required if 100+ employees and 50+ affected in single site within 30 days (with conditions)
- 60-day notice to affected employees, state, local government
- State mini-WARN laws have lower thresholds in CA, NY, NJ, others
- Penalty for violation: back pay + benefits for the notice period

## Workplace Investigations

When an issue arises that requires investigation:

**Investigation principles:**
- Prompt initiation
- Independent investigator (not in either party's chain)
- Privileged (in many cases, attorney-led for privilege protection)
- Documented
- Interim measures during investigation (separation, leave) — without prejudice
- Findings communicated; corrective action taken

**Common scenarios requiring formal investigation:**
- Harassment / discrimination complaints
- Workplace violence threats
- Financial misconduct (expense fraud, conflicts of interest, theft)
- Compliance violations (especially in regulated industries)
- Executive misconduct (always handle with board awareness)

**Document discipline:**
- Investigation notes are discoverable in litigation
- Write factually, avoid speculation or characterization
- Conclusions should be supported by documented evidence
- Privilege protections require careful handling — typically requires attorney involvement

## Privacy in the Workplace

**Monitoring employees:**
- Email, Slack, computer usage: generally permissible if disclosed in policy
- Some states require explicit consent (CT, DE)
- EU GDPR: significant additional protections; monitoring requires legal basis
- Personal devices used for work: extra care; BYOD policies require thought

**Personnel records:**
- Many states require employee access to their own personnel files
- Some require employer to maintain records for specific periods after termination
- Reference policies: stick to dates of employment + position unless authorized otherwise

**Social media:**
- Cannot ask for personal social media passwords (illegal in growing list of states)
- Can review public social media for hiring (but document non-discriminatory use)
- Discipline for off-duty social media speech is risky — NLRA-protected concerted activity, free speech in public employment, off-duty conduct laws

## When to Call Employment Counsel

Immediately:
- Discrimination, harassment, or retaliation complaint (especially in writing)
- Threat of litigation (lawyer letter, EEOC charge)
- Workplace injury beyond minor
- Termination of a member of a protected class with no documented performance history
- Mass layoffs (any size in regulated jurisdictions; >50 affected in US)
- Executive misconduct
- Suspected fraud or theft

Better to over-call than under-call. The cost of legal consultation is trivial compared to the cost of mishandled employment matters.

## The Manager Self-Check

Before any consequential employee action (hire, fire, demote, restructure):
1. Is the decision based on documented, job-related criteria?
2. Is the treatment consistent with similar past situations?
3. Have I considered whether the affected employee is in a protected class or recently engaged in protected activity?
4. Is the decision documented?
5. Has HR (and where appropriate, legal) reviewed?

If you can't answer yes to all five, pause. The pause costs hours; the alternative costs years.
