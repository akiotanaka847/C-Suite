# M&A Legal Essentials

*Note: General educational information, not legal advice. M&A transactions are highly fact-specific and jurisdiction-dependent. Engage experienced M&A counsel early.*

The legal work in M&A is where deals get won, lost, and rebuilt. The terms negotiated in the definitive agreement determine outcomes years after close. This is operator-level awareness — what to flag, what to push on, what counsel should be earning their fees doing.

## The Deal Documents

For an acquisition of a private company:

**Letter of Intent (LOI) / Term Sheet:**
- Non-binding except for specific clauses (exclusivity, confidentiality, expense reimbursement)
- Sets headline economic terms (price, structure, conditions)
- Usually 4-12 weeks of exclusivity
- Get this right — re-trading from LOI is bad-faith and burns goodwill

**Definitive Agreement (Stock Purchase Agreement / Asset Purchase Agreement / Merger Agreement):**
- The actual binding contract
- 80-200+ pages typically
- Negotiated over 6-12 weeks
- Where the real legal work happens

**Disclosure Schedules:**
- Exceptions to seller's representations and warranties
- "Section 3.5(b) of the SPA: Seller has no material contracts with terms greater than 5 years" — disclosure schedule lists any such contracts as exceptions
- Massive document — often longer than the SPA itself
- Critical for limiting seller liability

**Ancillary Agreements:**
- Employment / consulting agreements for retained executives
- Non-competition / non-solicitation agreements
- Escrow agreement (for the indemnification escrow)
- Transition services agreement (TSA) if applicable

## Stock vs. Asset Purchase

The structural choice has major tax and liability implications.

**Stock Purchase:**
- Buyer acquires the equity of the target company
- All assets AND liabilities transfer (including unknown liabilities)
- Generally less buyer-favorable from a liability standpoint
- Simpler from operational standpoint (no asset re-titling)
- Tax: buyer doesn't get step-up in asset basis; existing tax attributes (NOLs) usually carry over with limitations

**Asset Purchase:**
- Buyer acquires specific assets, expressly assumes specific liabilities
- Unassumed liabilities stay with the seller (in theory; statutory exceptions exist)
- More buyer-favorable for liability
- Operationally more complex (assigning contracts, re-titling assets, dealing with customer/vendor consents)
- Tax: buyer gets step-up in basis = future depreciation/amortization deductions

**Merger:**
- Statutory transaction; target merges into buyer (or buyer subsidiary)
- Reverse triangular merger most common (target becomes wholly-owned sub of buyer)
- Tax-free reorganization possible if structured correctly
- Often used when stock-for-stock consideration

**Typical use:**
- Small acquisitions / acquihires: asset purchase
- Larger strategic acquisitions: stock purchase or reverse triangular merger
- Public-to-public deals: merger structure

## Representations and Warranties

Statements by the seller about the company. If false, the buyer has a claim for breach.

**Fundamental representations (high stakes):**
- Authority and enforceability
- Capitalization (the cap table is what we represent)
- Title to assets / shares
- Tax (no undisclosed tax liabilities)

**Business representations:**
- Financial statements accuracy
- No material adverse changes since the last balance sheet
- Compliance with laws
- Material contracts (listed, assignable)
- IP ownership (no undisclosed third-party claims)
- Employees and benefits
- Litigation (disclosed; no undisclosed)
- Customers and suppliers (no material loss expected)
- Environmental (compliance, no liability)
- Privacy and data security (compliant, no breaches)

**Reps "scrubbed" via disclosure schedules:**
- Each rep can be limited by specifically disclosed exceptions
- Disclosure schedule is the seller's friend; the more disclosed, the less liability
- Buyers push for "schedule by exception" — only list exceptions to the rep; sellers push for more comprehensive disclosure

**Knowledge qualifiers:**
- "Seller has no knowledge of any pending or threatened claims"
- vs. simple "no pending or threatened claims"
- Knowledge qualifiers favor seller; absence favors buyer
- Definition of "knowledge" (actual? constructive? specific people?) negotiated heavily

## Indemnification

Buyer's remedy for breach of reps and warranties (and certain other matters).

**Components:**
- *Survival period*: how long reps survive close (typically 18-24 months for general reps; longer for fundamental)
- *Caps*: maximum total indemnification (typically 10-20% of purchase price for general reps; potentially uncapped for fundamental, fraud, etc.)
- *Baskets*: deductible before claims can be made (typically 0.5-1% of purchase price)
- *Escrow*: portion of purchase price held back to fund indemnification (typically 5-15% of price, released over 12-24 months)

**The negotiated economics:**
- Cap × probability of breach = expected liability for seller
- Buyer wants high cap, low basket, long survival, big escrow
- Seller wants low cap, high basket, short survival, small escrow
- Final terms reflect leverage and risk allocation

**Rep & Warranty Insurance:**
- Third-party insurance covering reps and warranties breaches
- Increasingly standard in middle-market deals
- Seller releases from indemnification (or has very limited indemnification)
- Buyer pursues insurer rather than seller for claims
- Cost: 2-4% of policy limit + premium
- Allows clean exit for seller; reduces buyer reliance on seller's collectability

## Closing Conditions

The buyer doesn't have to close unless conditions are met.

**Standard closing conditions:**
- Reps and warranties true at close (with materiality qualifier)
- Covenants performed (seller's promises about operating in the ordinary course pending close)
- No material adverse change (MAC) — significant negative event affecting the business
- Required regulatory approvals obtained (HSR antitrust, sector-specific)
- Required third-party consents obtained (key customers, lenders, landlords)
- Required employee retention (key person agreements signed; sometimes a minimum retention rate)

**Material Adverse Change (MAC):**
- Heavily negotiated. What counts as a MAC?
- Carve-outs: industry-wide events, general economic conditions, public-market changes, things buyer was aware of, target's own actions taken at buyer's request
- The classic MAC litigation: 2020 COVID cases (Tiffany v. LVMH, etc.) — buyers tried to walk; mostly settled at reduced prices

## Earnouts

A portion of consideration tied to post-close performance.

**Why earnouts exist:**
- Bridges valuation gaps when buyer and seller disagree on future performance
- Reduces buyer downside if performance doesn't materialize
- Aligns sellers (often staying in the business post-close) with continued success

**Earnout structures:**
- Revenue-based: easiest to measure, hardest to game
- EBITDA-based: more controllable by buyer post-close (allocation decisions, OpEx classifications)
- Milestone-based: discrete events (regulatory approval, product launch, customer signing)
- Time-based: tenure-related (employee earnout if certain people stay)

**Common earnout fights:**
- Post-close operating decisions that affect earnout metrics ("the new parent re-allocated marketing budget, hurting our segment")
- Accounting methodology changes
- Working capital adjustments that affect EBITDA
- M&A activity during earnout period

**Mitigation:**
- Define metrics precisely
- Restrict buyer's ability to make changes that affect metrics
- Cap or floor the earnout
- Clear dispute resolution mechanism (binding accountant, arbitration)

## Due Diligence Legal Issues

Where buyers find the problems that change deals:

**IP issues (very common):**
- Contractor work product not assigned to the company
- Open-source license obligations creating proprietary product risk
- Employees who developed IP at a previous company that's used here
- Trademarks not registered or in conflict with prior users

**Contract issues:**
- Change-of-control clauses requiring customer consent
- Most-Favored Nation (MFN) clauses locking pricing
- Exclusive arrangements limiting future flexibility
- Auto-renewal clauses creating unanticipated obligations

**Employment issues:**
- Misclassified contractors (should have been employees)
- Equity grants not properly documented
- Promised but undocumented bonuses or commissions
- Foreign employees with immigration status issues
- 280G "golden parachute" tax issues for executives

**Tax issues:**
- Unclaimed sales tax obligations (especially post-Wayfair)
- International transfer pricing
- R&D credits taken without sufficient documentation
- State income tax filings missed in nexus states

**Data privacy issues:**
- GDPR / CCPA compliance gaps
- Past breaches not properly notified
- Customer data used for unauthorized purposes

**Each issue:** either gets disclosed (and priced into the deal), repaired pre-close (delaying close), or covered by special indemnification.

## Regulatory Approvals

**Antitrust (US):**
- Hart-Scott-Rodino Act: transactions over a size threshold (adjusted annually; ~$120M+ in 2024) require pre-merger notification
- 30-day waiting period; can be extended via "second request" (deep investigation)
- Most filings clear without issue; second requests take 6-12+ months

**Antitrust (other jurisdictions):**
- EU merger control: substantial review for large deals
- UK, China, Japan, others have their own regimes
- Multi-jurisdictional deals can require parallel filings

**Sector-specific:**
- CFIUS (foreign investment in US): mandatory for certain critical technology / infrastructure
- Banking, insurance, healthcare, defense: sector regulators
- International: data localization, foreign ownership restrictions

## Common Deal-Killing Issues

**The deal is at risk if you find:**
- Undisclosed material litigation
- IP fundamental to the product owned by someone outside the company
- Compliance violations with criminal exposure
- Significant tax exposure (especially with personal liability)
- Major customer concentration with weak relationships
- Cultural incompatibility revealed in diligence
- Significant differences between disclosed and discovered financials

When these surface, the path is: repair, re-price, or walk. The honest decision is sometimes walking.

## Operational Discipline

For any deal in process:

**Negotiation hygiene:**
- All material conversations summarized in writing
- Key terms tracked in a deal points summary
- LOI changes vs. SPA changes flagged explicitly
- Communications with counsel privileged — handle accordingly

**Information sharing:**
- Clean room procedures for competitively sensitive information (separate access)
- Need-to-know basis on the deal team
- NDAs in place before any substantive sharing
- Consider sequencing — share less sensitive info first, more later as deal advances

**Post-close transition:**
- Day-1 communication plan executed precisely
- Required filings (HSR, securities, employment notifications) on time
- Working capital true-up usually 60-90 days post-close
- First indemnification claims often filed within 6 months

## The M&A Legal Diagnostic

For any deal you're considering:
1. Have we engaged experienced M&A counsel — not generalists?
2. What are the 3-5 most consequential terms (cap, basket, survival, escrow, MAC definition)?
3. What did diligence surface that affects deal terms?
4. Who will own the indemnification monitoring post-close?
5. What's our walk-away point and what are the terms triggers?

The deal will succeed or fail based on choices made in the documents. Pay attention to the boring parts; they're where money lives.
