# Talent Development and Succession

The companies that produce the most successful executives didn't get lucky. They built systems to develop and identify them, deliberately, over years. The companies that constantly hire from outside because "we don't have anyone internal" failed at this work — usually invisibly, over a long time.

## The Developmental Stack

A complete talent development program has four layers. Most companies do one or two and call it development.

**1. On-the-job experiences (70% of development)**
- Stretch assignments
- New role rotations
- Project leadership
- Customer-facing exposure
- Crisis management opportunities

**2. Coaching and feedback (20%)**
- Manager 1:1s
- Mentoring relationships
- Peer feedback
- 360-degree reviews

**3. Formal training (10%)**
- Skill-specific (negotiation, finance for non-financial managers, public speaking)
- Leadership programs
- Industry certifications
- Conferences and external programs

The "70-20-10" framework (popularized by CCL): the bulk of development comes from doing real work in stretch situations. Formal training matters but is the smallest piece.

## High-Potential Identification (HiPo)

Identify high-potential employees deliberately — early — and invest in them disproportionately.

**HiPo definition** (not just high performance):
- *High performance* in current role (baseline requirement)
- *Demonstrated learning agility* — handles novel situations well; takes feedback and grows
- *Aspiration* for broader scope
- *Capability to grow* into roles 1-2 levels above current

Note: high performance ≠ high potential. Some excellent ICs are excellent in this role specifically and not built for more scope. That's not a failure — promoting them is the failure.

**HiPo identification process:**
- Annual talent review with leadership team
- Discuss each employee at director-and-above level
- 9-box matrix (performance × potential) is a common framing tool
- Document the calibration; revisit annually

**The 9-box matrix:**

| | Low Performance | Medium Performance | High Performance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **High Potential** | Coach urgently | Stretch | Top talent |
| **Medium Potential** | Performance issue | Solid contributor | Strong performer |
| **Low Potential** | Manage out | Specialist | Specialist |

**Common HiPo program elements:**
- Special projects exposure
- Senior executive sponsorship (not the same as their manager)
- Accelerated development plan
- Cross-functional rotations
- Increased visibility (presenting to board, leadership meetings)

**The HiPo trap:**
- HiPo label becomes self-fulfilling — they get attention, opportunities, growth
- Non-HiPos see the system as rigged, disengage
- Cure: review the HiPo list annually with fresh eyes; demote from the list as well as promote in

## Succession Planning

Succession planning is identifying who could take key roles if the current incumbent leaves (or is promoted, or is incapacitated).

**Per critical role, document:**
- *Ready now*: 1-3 candidates who could step in immediately
- *Ready in 1-2 years*: 2-4 candidates who could step in with development
- *Watch list*: emerging talent who could be candidates in 3-5 years

**Critical roles to plan for:**
- All C-suite roles
- All VPs with significant team size
- All roles where the incumbent has critical relationships (top customers, key investors, regulators)
- Any role where unexpected loss would damage the business in <90 days

**The "hit by a bus" test:**
- For each leader, who covers their work tomorrow if they're suddenly unavailable?
- If the answer is "no one," that's a critical succession gap
- Document the answer; test it (e.g., during leader's vacation)

**Build-vs-buy decision per role:**
- Strong internal candidate: build (cheaper, cultural continuity, faster ramp)
- No internal candidate, role is critical: start build (mentor a potential successor) AND identify external option
- Role requires fresh perspective (turnaround, new strategic direction): often best to buy

## Career Pathing

People want to know how their career grows. Most companies fail to make this explicit and lose top talent to companies that do.

**Two parallel tracks (the standard for tech):**
- *Individual contributor (IC) track*: IC1 → Senior IC → Staff → Principal → Distinguished
- *Manager track*: Manager → Senior Manager → Director → Senior Director → VP → SVP

**Equivalence at each level:**
- Staff IC ≈ Senior Manager (comp, scope, impact)
- Principal IC ≈ Director or Senior Director
- Distinguished IC ≈ VP or SVP

The equivalence matters. Without it, IC track becomes a career dead-end and your best engineers/designers/operators leave for companies that respect it.

**Promotion criteria per level:**
- Documented scope expectations
- Behavioral indicators of operating at the next level
- Required impact demonstrations
- Calibration across managers (someone at this level here is comparable to someone at this level across the company)

**The promotion process:**
- Manager nominates with written case
- Calibration discussion across managers in the function
- Cross-functional calibration for senior levels
- Promotion communicated with clear rationale

**Lateral moves:**
- Often more developmental than vertical promotions
- New function exposes the employee to different work; expands network
- Lateral moves should NOT be a demotion in disguise (different problem)

## Leadership Pipeline (Charan)

Each transition in a leadership career requires a fundamental shift in skills, time allocation, and identity. Companies that under-invest in transition support produce leaders who failed at the previous level and got promoted anyway.

**The six passages:**

1. *Manage self → Manage others*: shift from doing the work to enabling the work
2. *Manage others → Manage managers*: shift from operational to organizational
3. *Manage managers → Functional manager*: shift from one function's depth to function-wide thinking
4. *Functional manager → Business manager*: shift from function to general management (P&L)
5. *Business manager → Group manager*: shift from one business to portfolio of businesses
6. *Group manager → Enterprise manager (CEO)*: shift from operating businesses to setting enterprise direction

**Each passage requires:**
- New skills (often opposed to what worked at the previous level)
- New time allocation (less doing, more coaching, planning, communicating)
- New values (less personal accomplishment, more enabling others' accomplishment)

**Symptoms of failed passage:**
- A manager-of-managers still micromanaging individual contributors
- A functional VP optimizing only their function (not the cross-functional outcome)
- A CEO still operating like a COO

The cure is recognizing the passage requirement explicitly, providing coaching during the transition, and resetting expectations on what success looks like at the new level.

## Learning and Development Programs

Formal L&D should be the smallest piece — but it's the most institutionally visible.

**Effective programs:**
- Just-in-time learning tied to a real situation (negotiation training right before a major contract negotiation)
- Cohort programs with peer learning (executive education, leadership development programs)
- Coaching (executive coach for senior leaders)
- Internal teaching (a subject matter expert teaches a course — develops them AND the audience)

**Ineffective programs:**
- Annual mandatory training that everyone clicks through
- "Soft skills" workshops disconnected from real work
- Generic online learning platforms with low completion rates
- Conferences attended without follow-up

**The L&D budget conversation:**
- Most companies under-spend on senior leader development (where ROI is highest)
- Most companies over-spend on entry-level training (where impact decays fastest)
- The right allocation is heavily weighted toward emerging and senior leaders

## When to Manage Out

A talent development program also includes the discipline of moving people out.

**The decision tree:**
1. *Performance gap* — is the gap actionable? Can it close in 60-90 days with focused support?
2. *Right person, wrong role?* — would they succeed in a different role at the company? Move them.
3. *Wrong person?* — they will not succeed here. Manage out with dignity and severance.
4. *Right person, right role, wrong moment?* — sometimes a life event is the cause. Patience for a defined period.

**Cost of not managing out:**
- Lost productivity from the underperformer
- Lost productivity from peers compensating
- Cultural signal that performance doesn't matter
- A-players leave because they're not surrounded by A-players

The cost compounds. Most "managing out" decisions should have been made 3-6 months earlier.

## The Talent Diagnostic

Annually, leadership team should answer:
1. Who are our top 10% performers? Are they being challenged and growing?
2. Who's our succession for each C-suite role and key VP? Ready when? Are we developing them?
3. Who's on the "should have moved on" list? What's the plan?
4. What % of our director-and-above hires last year were internal vs. external?
5. What development experience are we giving HiPos this year?

If you can't answer #2 for critical roles, you have a succession gap that becomes visible only when it's too late.
