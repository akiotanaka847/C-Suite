# Investor Relations

Investor relations is the practice of building and maintaining trust with the people who funded your company. The IR work that matters happens between board meetings, in the small communications that compound into either trust or skepticism.

## The Two Audiences

**Board investors** — those holding board seats. Active, voting, with information rights. Their relationship is governance-shaped (see `board_communication.md` and `board_composition_and_governance.md`).

**Non-board investors** — angels, smaller funds, advisor-investors, secondary holders. No vote, no formal information rights beyond what they negotiated, but they:
- Talk to other investors about you
- Refer customers and talent
- Sometimes hold meaningful equity
- Can become loud in down scenarios

The mistake most CEOs make: treating non-board investors as irrelevant until they need them. By the time you need them, the relationship is cold.

## The Monthly Investor Update — Your Highest-Leverage IR Tool

A short monthly email to all investors (board + non-board) is the single most effective IR practice.

**Benefits:**
- Reduces the time spent in board meetings on backward-looking updates
- Surfaces help and intros (asks) you wouldn't otherwise get
- Builds the reflex of regular, honest communication
- Creates a paper trail of trajectory and decisions

**Format (see also `skills/board/board-update-memo.md`):**
- TL;DR (3 sentences)
- Key metrics table (8-10 metrics with trends)
- What went well (3 bullets)
- What didn't (3 bullets — the trust-builder)
- Asks (1-3, specific and time-bound)
- Looking ahead (one paragraph)

**Total length:** 600-900 words. Longer than that and people stop reading.

**Cadence:** monthly is the norm for growth-stage. Quarterly is acceptable post-Series-B if monthly fatigue sets in.

**Tone:** direct, numerical, vulnerable when appropriate. The CEO who only sends "everything is great" updates trains investors to discount their communications.

## What to Communicate Between Updates

Material developments should not wait for the monthly. Reach out individually for:

**Reach out same-day:**
- Loss of a top-3 customer
- Departure of a key executive (especially before public knowledge)
- Material breach or security incident
- Press inquiry on a sensitive topic
- Major acquisition offer or strategic conversation

**Reach out within a week:**
- Hiring a senior executive
- Signing a top-10 customer
- Material product milestone
- Pricing change announcement
- Office or geographic expansion

**Wait for the monthly:**
- Routine wins and losses
- Operational metric movements within range
- Internal organizational changes below VP level

**The rule:** investors hate surprises more than they hate bad news. The CEO who calls before the bad news leaks earns trust. The CEO who lets investors find out from a customer or a TechCrunch article spends years rebuilding.

## Managing the Investor Communication Channel

**Email:**
- Use a dedicated investor email list (e.g., `investors@company.com` BCC list)
- BCC, not CC — investors shouldn't be cross-emailing each other from your update
- Investor-only Slack channels rarely work — too noisy, too easily mismanaged

**Quarterly investor calls:**
- For companies with many investors (20+), a quarterly call substitutes for individual updates
- 30-45 minutes: CEO presents top-of-mind topics, Q&A
- Recorded and shared for investors who can't attend live

**Investor portals:**
- Carta, AngelList, Visible.vc, etc. enable shared documents and metrics
- Useful for transparency and reducing repeated info requests
- Don't replace personal communication — investors who matter want to hear from the CEO

## Asks — The Underutilized IR Lever

Most CEOs underuse the ask muscle. Investors signed up to help. Specific asks get help; vague ones don't.

**Effective asks:**
- "Looking for warm intros to CMOs at $100M+ B2B SaaS companies for a VP Marketing hire — would value any names you'd refer"
- "Considering Sequoia as Series B lead — would appreciate honest perspective from anyone who has worked with them"
- "Doing customer dev on AI agents in the legal vertical — would love intros to GCs at PE-backed companies"

**Ineffective asks:**
- "Always looking for great people"
- "Any leads welcome"
- "Would love feedback on our deck" (without specific area)

Track which investors deliver on asks. The ones who do are your high-value relationships; the ones who don't are noise.

## Pro-Rata, Follow-On, and Future Rounds

Investor relationships shape your next round.

**Pro-rata rights:**
- The right to invest enough in subsequent rounds to maintain ownership %
- Typically granted to lead investors in each round
- Honor the rights even when you don't have to — burning relationships over pro-rata is short-sighted

**Insider follow-ons:**
- Existing investors leading or co-leading the next round
- Signals confidence to new investors
- Avoid "insider-only" rounds (signals to new investors that no outside party would lead) — try to get at least one new lead

**Bridge financing:**
- Convertible notes or SAFEs between priced rounds
- Common, but signals difficulty raising at a new mark
- Insider-led bridges are healthier than third-party bridges (insiders bet on themselves)
- Negotiate caps and discounts as you would any priced round

## Down Rounds and Hard Conversations

When the next round is at a lower valuation than the last, IR becomes critical.

**Pre-down-round preparation:**
- Communicate the market reality early (3-6 months ahead) so it's not a surprise
- Frame the decision as "raising more at lower valuation > raising less at high valuation" if the company needs capital
- Pre-discuss anti-dilution mechanics (existing investors get more shares to compensate for valuation drop)
- Pre-discuss option pool refresh and employee impact

**Day-of communication:**
- Lead with the operating logic, not the financial result
- Be honest about why the market shifted (or why the company underperformed)
- Address employee impact specifically (option pool, refresh grants, communication to team)

**Investor pushback to expect:**
- "Why didn't we cut burn sooner?"
- "What's different about the plan now?"
- "Why is this the right time to raise vs. cutting further and waiting?"

Answer these proactively. The CEO who can articulate them clearly demonstrates judgment; the CEO who avoids them loses confidence.

## Secondary Sales

Existing shareholders selling shares to new investors — increasingly common for late-stage companies.

**For founder secondaries:**
- Boards and lead investors typically approve a fixed % of founder stake (5-15%) at major rounds
- Reduces the financial pressure on founders, lets them focus long-term
- Excessive secondaries (>20%) signal the founder is losing belief

**For employee secondaries:**
- Tender offers for vested options or RSUs
- Provides liquidity without IPO
- Often run by the company or by a third-party (Forge, Carta) at scheduled windows

**Communication implications:**
- Secondary pricing is public-ish (becomes known among investors)
- A secondary at a discount to the recent primary signals concern
- Manage messaging around the discount (usually framed as "illiquidity discount")

## When You're Acquired

The acquisition process is intensive IR work compressed into 3-6 months.

**Investor communication during a process:**
- Inform board at the LOI stage; broader investor base at definitive agreement (or earlier per agreement with the board)
- Smaller investors learn at announcement
- Be prepared for "why didn't you tell me earlier?" — explain the confidentiality requirements
- Communicate the outcome math clearly (preference stack, common share proceeds, retention packages)

**Post-close investor relations:**
- Final report on outcomes per share class
- Tax documents (1099-DIV or appropriate equivalent)
- Often a final email thanking investors and the relationships

A clean acquisition wrap-up sets you up for future fundraising — investors who had a good experience invest in your next company.

## Common IR Failures

1. **Silence in hard quarters** — disappearing when things are bad. Investors fill the silence with worst-case assumptions.
2. **Manufactured asks** — generic asks to look engaged. Investors stop reading.
3. **Cherry-picked metrics** — reporting metrics that paint a rosy picture, omitting the ones that look bad. Investors notice; trust erodes.
4. **Different stories to different investors** — telling one investor about a pivot and another that everything is on plan. Investors talk.
5. **Treating non-board investors as second-class** — they remember when they had no voice. Cold relationships when you need them.
6. **Not running a real fundraising process** — taking the first term sheet from an existing investor without testing the market. Often leaves significant valuation on the table.

## The IR Diagnostic

Once a quarter, ask:
1. When did I last reach out individually to each board member? Each non-board investor?
2. Which investors are delivering value (intros, advice, hires)? Which aren't?
3. What's my next major fundraise timeline, and which existing investors will lead or co-lead?
4. What's the worst thing my biggest investor would say about me right now, if they were being honest?
5. What's the one piece of bad news I haven't yet told the board?

Answer #5 honestly. That's the IR debt that's compounding. Pay it down before it gets bigger.
