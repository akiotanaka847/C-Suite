# Product Launch Playbooks

A launch is not a date — it's a campaign. The companies that launch well treat it as a 6-12 week operation with deliberate phasing, cross-functional alignment, and a measurable success definition. The companies that don't ship features that nobody adopts.

## Launch Tiers — Not All Launches Are Equal

Most product orgs treat every release the same way. They shouldn't.

**Tier 1: Major launches (1-3 per year)**
- New products, major version releases, category-defining features
- Full marketing investment: PR, content campaign, sales enablement, customer comms
- Cross-functional swarm; executive engagement
- Launch day is a moment in the calendar

**Tier 2: Feature launches (4-12 per year)**
- Significant new capabilities that customers will care about
- Content + email + in-product announcement
- Sales enablement for talking points
- Soft launch + measurable adoption goals

**Tier 3: Incremental releases (continuous)**
- Improvements, bug fixes, small additions
- Release notes; in-product callout if appropriate
- No marketing campaign; minimal customer comms

**The mistake:** treating all releases as Tier 1 burns the marketing team and trains customers to ignore announcements. Treating all releases as Tier 3 means major launches don't get the support they need.

**The discipline:** explicit tiering at planning time; budget and resource allocated accordingly.

## The 6-Week Launch Timeline (Tier 1)

**Week -6: Foundation**
- Launch goals defined (adoption, revenue, pipeline, awareness — pick 2-3)
- Audience defined (target customers, target segments)
- Messaging finalized (positioning statement, top 3 value props, proof)
- Launch leader named with cross-functional team
- Customer beta program complete with testimonials in hand

**Week -4: Asset development**
- Press release / blog post drafted
- Customer case studies / testimonials finalized
- Sales enablement materials (deck, FAQ, battle card vs. competitors)
- Demo updated and recorded
- Landing page built
- Email campaigns drafted
- Social copy drafted

**Week -2: Pre-launch outreach**
- Press briefings (under embargo) — 5-15 publications
- Analyst briefings (Gartner, Forrester, etc.)
- Customer pre-briefing (top accounts get advance notice)
- Partner notification (integration partners, channel)
- Internal alignment all-hands

**Week -1: Final prep**
- Sales team training session
- Customer success team training
- Support team training (anticipate questions, document answers)
- Final asset review
- Launch day runbook drafted

**Week 0: Launch day**
- Press release goes out (typically 6am ET)
- Blog post and website update live
- Email to existing customers
- Social campaign begins
- Customer outreach (top accounts get personal note from CSM or AM)
- Internal celebration
- Active monitoring (PR pickup, social, support tickets)

**Week +1-2: Sustain**
- Follow-up content (deeper-dive on features, customer stories)
- Sales outreach to qualified inbound
- Webinar or demo session
- Customer engagement (in-product nudges to try the new feature)

**Week +4: Retrospective**
- What worked, what didn't
- Adoption metrics review
- Lessons documented for next launch

## Cross-Functional Roles

A launch is failure-by-default if no one owns it end-to-end.

**Launch Leader:**
- Product marketing manager (most common)
- Single throat-to-choke
- Owns the timeline, the goals, the cross-functional alignment

**Product:**
- Owns the product itself shipping on time
- Owns the feature documentation
- Available for customer / press briefings

**Engineering:**
- Ensures readiness (testing, scalability, monitoring)
- On-call coverage for launch period
- Bug-fix capacity during launch

**Marketing (broader):**
- Content campaign
- Email and lifecycle
- Digital ads (sometimes)
- PR / analyst relations
- Brand voice

**Sales:**
- Enablement materials
- Pipeline targets tied to launch
- Customer outreach

**Customer Success:**
- Customer notification cadence
- Adoption support
- Account-level rollouts for enterprise customers

**Support:**
- Documentation
- Training to handle launch-related questions
- Escalation paths for launch issues

**Legal:**
- Press release review
- Customer comms review
- Trademark considerations (for new product names)

The launch leader pulls the team together; each function owns their piece.

## Launch Goals — Define Success Before Launching

A launch without clear goals is a launch you can't evaluate.

**Goal types:**

*Adoption:*
- N% of eligible customers adopt the feature within 30 days
- N users active in the new capability within 60 days
- N% of new signups use the feature in first session

*Revenue:*
- $X in new ARR from the launch within 90 days
- N% of new customers upgrade to the tier containing the feature
- N expansion deals tied to the feature

*Pipeline:*
- N qualified leads generated from the launch
- N demos booked
- N new logos in the target segment

*Awareness:*
- N press pickups (with quality threshold — Tier 1 publications)
- Branded search lift
- Social engagement (with quality threshold — meaningful comments, not just likes)

**Pick 2-3 goals.** More than that and the team can't prioritize. Less than that and you can't tell if you succeeded.

## Press and Analyst Relations

For Tier 1 launches.

**Press strategy:**
- Pre-briefing 5-15 publications under embargo
- Tier 1: target trade press in your category, business press (Bloomberg, WSJ, etc.), specific writers known to cover the space
- Tier 2: write your own — owned blog post, LinkedIn long-form, X thread
- Goal: 3-5 quality articles on launch day plus a dozen smaller mentions

**Embargo discipline:**
- Embargo time and date in every briefing
- "Embargo" must be agreed to (some publications won't honor)
- Track who's been briefed; follow up after launch for coverage

**Analyst relations:**
- Brief Gartner, Forrester, IDC, etc. in advance
- Goal: inclusion in research reports, market maps, vendor evaluations
- Long-cycle: analysts move slowly; relationship investment is multi-year

**The PR reality:**
- Most launches don't get major press coverage
- That's OK — owned channels (your blog, email, social) can be enough
- Don't gate launch success on press pickup

## Customer Communication

How to tell existing customers about a launch.

**Tiered approach:**

*Top customers (top 5-10%):*
- Personal note from AM or CSM in advance
- Early access if appropriate
- Custom briefing on what it means for them
- Quote-able testimonial post-launch (if relevant)

*Mid-tier customers (next 30-40%):*
- Pre-launch email a few days before
- Webinar invitation
- In-product announcement

*Long-tail customers:*
- Launch-day email
- In-product announcement
- Self-service docs

**The customer comms goal:**
- Existing customers feel cared for
- Adoption of the new capability where relevant
- Avoid: launch that feels like surprise to customers ("when did this change?")

## Sales Enablement

For Tier 1 launches, sales must be able to talk about the new capability confidently within hours of launch.

**Materials needed:**

- *Positioning summary*: 1-page overview with messaging
- *FAQ*: top 20 questions sales will get
- *Competitive battle card*: how to position vs. competitors who might also be in the space
- *Demo updates*: refreshed demo with the new capability
- *Sales deck updates*: slide refreshes reflecting the new offering
- *Pricing guidance*: what's included in which tier, any pricing changes

**Training format:**
- 30-60 minute training session
- Recorded for new hires
- Required attendance for all customer-facing roles
- Q&A captured and added to FAQ

**Customer-facing slides:**
- Update within 1 week of launch
- Notify all reps; require update of personal decks

## Tracking the Launch

In the days and weeks after launch:

**Daily (first week):**
- PR pickup count and quality
- Website traffic to launch page
- Sign-ups attributed to launch
- Adoption metric (whatever the goal was)
- Support ticket volume related to launch
- Sales-team feedback (anecdotal)

**Weekly (first month):**
- Adoption trend
- Pipeline attributed to launch
- Customer feedback themes (from support, CS, sales)
- Press mention quality

**Monthly (first quarter):**
- Revenue attribution
- Retention of users who adopted the new capability
- ROI vs. launch investment
- Lessons for next launch

## Launch Retrospective

Within 4 weeks of launch, the team retrospective:

**What worked:**
- Goals achieved or exceeded
- Specific tactics that performed above expectation
- Cross-functional collaboration wins

**What didn't:**
- Goals missed and why
- Tactics that underperformed
- Process or coordination breakdowns

**What to do differently next time:**
- Specific changes for the next Tier 1 launch
- Process improvements
- Asset reuse and improvement

**Document and share:**
- Internal blog post or doc
- Next launch leader has reference material
- Patterns surface over multiple launches

## Common Launch Failures

1. **Launching without a beta program** — no customer proof, no testimonials, no early signal
2. **Treating it as a press release** — write the release, ship the feature, expect adoption
3. **Sales surprised by launch** — sees the email from marketing, hasn't been trained
4. **No adoption goal** — launches "successfully" but the feature has zero users in 90 days
5. **Over-promising** — claims exceed product reality; trust damage
6. **Launch fatigue** — too many launches, sales and customers tune out
7. **No retrospective** — same mistakes repeat across launches
8. **Coordination chaos** — multiple owners; messaging inconsistent across channels

## The Launch Diagnostic

For any upcoming launch, ask:
1. What tier is this launch — Tier 1, 2, or 3?
2. What are the 2-3 success goals — and how will we measure them?
3. Who's the single launch leader?
4. What's the beta or pre-launch customer engagement?
5. What's our plan if the launch underperforms?

#5 is the most-skipped question. Plans for the upside ("more pipeline! more revenue!") are easy. Plans for the downside (slow adoption, weak press) are where execution matters.
