# Uber Culture Crisis (2017)

## Situation

By 2016, Uber was the most valuable private company in the world, valued at $68 billion and operating in 83 countries. It had grown from a San Francisco startup to a global platform in six years. CEO Travis Kalanick operated under a set of internal cultural values that included "Always Be Hustlin'," "Meritocracy and Toe-Stepping," and "Let Builders Build." The culture rewarded aggressive growth and individual output. HR was structurally subordinate to business operations.

## What Happened

In February 2017, engineer Susan Fowler published a blog post describing systematic sexual harassment during her time at Uber, and HR's repeated failure to act on her complaints — including being told that a manager who had propositioned her would not be disciplined because it was "his first offense" and he was "a high performer." The post went viral. Within a week, Uber commissioned an independent investigation led by former US Attorney General Eric Holder. The investigation found: a toxic workplace culture, inadequate HR function, management retaliation against complainants, and a pattern of executives using their power inappropriately. 20 employees were fired as a result. Kalanick resigned as CEO in June 2017 under pressure from investors.

## Root Cause

The cultural values Uber institutionalized explicitly rewarded competitive, aggressive behavior and discouraged deference to process or hierarchy. HR was positioned as an obstacle to "builders," not as a function protecting employee rights or company risk. The result was that managers learned harassment complaints were unlikely to affect their standing if they were high performers. The system optimized for growth and suppressed feedback that threatened that optimization.

## Key Decision Failures

- **Cultural values that made aggression a virtue**: Written company values explicitly celebrated behaviors that, when applied to interpersonal conduct, created a hostile environment
- **HR subordinated to business operations**: HR lacked the organizational authority to act independently on complaints; it reported into lines that prioritized business performance over compliance
- **High-performer protection**: Repeated instances of HR declining to act because the offender was high-performing created an explicit policy that output justified misconduct
- **No anonymous reporting mechanism**: Employees who faced retaliation for complaints had no protected channel; the system required going to the same HR that had already failed them
- **CEO behavior modeled the culture**: Multiple accounts described Kalanick's personal conduct setting the cultural tone; leadership conduct is always the de facto policy

## Lessons

1. **Culture is what you tolerate, not what you write**: A values document that says "respect" while tolerating harassment communicates the opposite. The operative culture is revealed by how the organization responds when values conflict with performance.
2. **HR independence is a governance function**: HR that reports through business lines and is evaluated on business outcomes will always protect business over employees when the two conflict. Structural independence — including a direct line to the board — is required for HR to function as a control.
3. **High-performer exceptions teach everyone**: When one person is protected from consequences because they perform well, every other employee learns that performance overrides conduct standards. This is the policy, regardless of what the handbook says.
4. **The cost of a culture crisis exceeds the cost of the culture fix**: Uber's valuation, talent pipeline, regulatory relationships, and brand were damaged for years. Addressing the structural HR and values issues earlier would have been vastly cheaper.
