# Spirit Airlines Collapse (2024–2026)

## Situation

Spirit Airlines was the largest ultra-low-cost carrier (ULCC) in the United States, operating on a model of bare-bones fares supplemented by fees for every add-on: carry-on bags, seat selection, water. By 2022 Spirit had grown to 200+ aircraft and was positioned to merge with Frontier Airlines, a fellow ULCC, in a deal that would have created a scaled-up competitor to the major carriers. The combined entity's business case was explicit: reach the scale to sustain the ULCC cost structure against legacy carriers.

## What Happened

Frontier terminated its merger agreement in July 2022 after Spirit's board rejected it in favor of a competing bid from JetBlue. JetBlue offered a higher price per share. The DOJ sued to block the JetBlue-Spirit merger on antitrust grounds in January 2024. Federal courts sided with the DOJ and the merger was permanently enjoined. Spirit was left standing alone: no merger partner, no capital infusion, and a balance sheet weakened by pandemic losses and high-cost aircraft leases. Spirit filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in November 2024, emerged briefly, then filed again in 2025 and ceased operations in May 2026.

## Root Cause

The ULCC model depended on maintaining a structural cost advantage over legacy carriers — American, Delta, United — that allowed Spirit to undercut them on price and fill seats that would otherwise go empty. From 2022 onward that cost advantage eroded. Legacy carriers adopted unbundled pricing of their own, matching Spirit's fee structure while offering the reliability and loyalty program benefits Spirit could not. Spirit lost its differentiation. The Frontier merger would have provided scale to compete; the board abandoned it for a higher per-share offer that could not close. Operationally Spirit was left with a fleet of expensive leased aircraft, high fuel exposure, and a brand that customers associated with delays and discomfort.

## Key Decision Failures

- **Board prioritized acquisition premium over strategic necessity**: The Frontier deal was strategically coherent; the JetBlue deal was financially attractive but carried fatal regulatory risk — the DOJ had already signaled intent to challenge it. The board chose price over probability.
- **Antitrust risk was not adequately weighted**: JetBlue's offer was contingent on regulatory approval in a market where DOJ had blocked multiple airline consolidations. A rigorous risk-adjusted analysis of deal value would have returned the Frontier deal as superior.
- **No Plan B for merger failure**: Spirit's standalone financial model was built for growth, not for a multi-year regulatory battle followed by no deal. The company had no viable path to profitability as an independent carrier once legacy carriers matched its pricing model.
- **ULCC model not stress-tested against competitive response**: The business model assumed legacy carriers would not unbundle aggressively. They did. No structural advantage was sustainable once competitors adopted the same playbook.

## Lessons

1. **Strategic deals must be evaluated on risk-adjusted value, not headline price**: A higher per-share price on a deal with 40% probability of regulatory failure is worth less than a lower price on a deal with 90% probability of closing. Boards that conflate offer price with deal value make this mistake consistently.
2. **Cost-leadership strategy requires durable structural advantage**: A cost advantage that competitors can copy by changing their pricing page is not a moat. ULCC economics required scale that Spirit never achieved as a standalone carrier.
3. **When the strategic rationale is consolidation, standalone viability must be stress-tested**: If the business plan requires a merger to work, the board must know — and disclose — what happens if no deal closes.
