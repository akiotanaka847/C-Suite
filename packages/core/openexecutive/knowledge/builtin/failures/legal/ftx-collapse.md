# FTX Collapse (2022)

## Situation

FTX was a cryptocurrency exchange founded by Sam Bankman-Fried (SBF) in 2019. By 2022, FTX was the second-largest crypto exchange by volume, valued at $32 billion, with investments from Sequoia Capital, Temasek, Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan, and others. SBF was a prominent public figure, speaking at congressional hearings and donating to political campaigns. Alameda Research, a crypto trading firm also controlled by SBF, was a significant market maker.

## What Happened

In November 2022, a CoinDesk report revealed that a large portion of Alameda Research's assets consisted of FTT — FTX's own exchange token. Binance announced it would sell its FTT holdings. A bank-run dynamic ensued: FTX users attempted to withdraw approximately $6 billion in 72 hours. FTX could not process the withdrawals. FTX filed for bankruptcy on November 11, 2022. Subsequent investigation revealed FTX had transferred approximately $8 billion in customer funds to Alameda Research, which had used them for investments and to fund expenses. SBF was arrested, extradited to the United States, convicted of seven counts of fraud and conspiracy, and sentenced to 25 years in federal prison.

## Root Cause

FTX operated without the basic financial controls that any regulated institution requires. Customer funds were not segregated from company funds. Alameda Research had a special internal status that exempted it from FTX's standard liquidation protocols — allowing it to maintain large negative balances backed by customer assets. There was no CFO for most of FTX's existence, no independent board, no audit of the consolidated entity, and no functioning compliance program. The organizational structure was designed to concentrate control and eliminate accountability.

## Key Decision Failures

- **Customer funds not segregated**: The foundational requirement of any custodial financial institution — that customer assets cannot be used for company purposes — was violated from the beginning
- **No CFO, no audit, no board**: FTX lacked the three primary institutional structures that would have detected or prevented commingling: a CFO who could refuse unauthorized transfers, an auditor who would review them, and a board that would receive financial reports
- **Alameda given special system access**: FTX's code was modified to allow Alameda to maintain large negative balances — a technical backdoor that allowed the fund transfer to continue invisibly
- **Governance theater substituted for governance**: FTX's published terms of service described customer fund segregation that did not exist; regulatory appearances and political donations created the appearance of compliance without the substance
- **Investors did not conduct financial due diligence**: At $32 billion valuation, sophisticated institutional investors provided capital without reviewing consolidated financial statements or demanding audited accounts of customer fund segregation

## Lessons

1. **Customer fund segregation is binary**: A custodian that has access to customer funds will eventually use them. The only reliable protection is technical and legal segregation — separate accounts, separate systems, separate entities — that makes access structurally impossible without detection.
2. **Absence of basic corporate controls is a fraud risk signal, not a startup quirk**: No CFO, no board, no audit at $32 billion valuation is not an efficient startup. It is a structure that makes fraud possible and accountability impossible. Investors should treat missing controls as a deal-stopper.
3. **Regulatory appearances are not compliance**: Congressional testimony, political donations, and published terms of service describe a company as it presents itself. Financial controls describe a company as it operates. Verify the latter.
4. **At late-stage valuations, due diligence standards should match public company standards**: Institutional investors who fund a company at $32 billion without audited financials are extending trust where verification is available. The verification is the standard.
