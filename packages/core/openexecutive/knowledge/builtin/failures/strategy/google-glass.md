# Google Glass (2013)

## Situation

In 2013, Google launched Google Glass Explorer Edition — a head-mounted computer with a small display, camera, microphone, and internet connectivity — to a select group of early adopters ("Explorers") for $1,500. Google positioned it as the next computing platform: always-on, hands-free, augmented reality layered over daily life. The product received enormous media coverage. It was worn by celebrities and featured in fashion spreads.

## What Happened

The product failed publicly. "Glassholes" — a term users coined — were banned from bars, restaurants, and movie theatres. The always-on camera created genuine social anxiety about consent and surveillance. The $1,500 price excluded mass-market adoption. Battery life lasted four hours. The use cases (checking email, taking photos, navigation) were either served adequately by phones or created awkward social situations when done hands-free. Google quietly discontinued the Explorer Edition in January 2015, less than two years after launch.

## Root Cause

Google designed around a technology capability — a miniaturized connected display — without first validating a customer problem worth solving in public, social contexts. The internal question was "what can we do with this?" rather than "what problem does this solve better than existing alternatives?" The privacy concerns were foreseeable and were not resolved before launch; the product shipped into a social context it had not been designed for.

## Key Decision Failures

- **Technology-first, problem-second**: The product roadmap began with the hardware capability, not a validated user need
- **Ignoring social context**: A head-mounted camera in social settings creates consent and surveillance dynamics that a phone camera does not; this was not addressed in the product design
- **Public beta before product-market fit**: Launching to a high-profile "Explorer" cohort amplified the backlash; a quieter pilot with enterprise users (where Glass found genuine traction later) would have been lower risk
- **Price anchoring at aspirational consumer**: $1,500 positioned it as a premium consumer product, creating expectations the functionality couldn't meet
- **No clear ICP**: Glass was marketed to everyone — athletes, professionals, consumers — with no prioritized beachhead where it would win first

## Lessons

1. **Technology capability is not a product strategy**: "We can build this" is not the same as "someone needs this." Customer discovery must precede product development, not follow it.
2. **Social context is a product requirement**: Products used in social settings face adoption dynamics that private-use products don't. Design for the social contract, not just the use case.
3. **Quiet pilots beat splashy launches for unproven products**: Enterprise Glass found real traction in manufacturing and healthcare. A beachhead strategy — win one segment deeply — would have preserved the platform while building evidence.
4. **A failed consumer launch poisons subsequent market attempts**: The "Glasshole" reputation damaged every subsequent AR product Google announced.
