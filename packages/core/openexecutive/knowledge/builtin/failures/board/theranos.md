# Theranos Board Failure (2018)

## Situation

Theranos, founded in 2003 by Elizabeth Holmes, claimed to have developed a device — the "Edison" — that could run hundreds of diagnostic tests from a single finger-prick of blood. By 2013, Theranos was valued at $9 billion. Holmes appeared on magazine covers and was compared to Steve Jobs. Walgreens and Safeway entered major commercial partnerships. Theranos was processing patient blood tests in its wellness centers. Holmes served as CEO and Chair; the board included George Shultz (former US Secretary of State), Henry Kissinger, James Mattis, and other former generals and cabinet officials.

## What Happened

In 2015, journalist John Carreyrou at the Wall Street Journal published an investigation reporting that Theranos's Edison device could not perform most of the tests it claimed, and that Theranos was running patient samples on conventional third-party machines. Former employees described being threatened with legal action for speaking to journalists or regulators. The FDA subsequently found that the Edison was an "uncleared medical device." The CMS revoked Theranos's laboratory certification in 2016. Theranos was dissolved in 2018. Holmes was convicted of fraud in 2022 and sentenced to 11 years in federal prison.

## Root Cause

The Theranos board never commissioned or received independent technical validation of the core product claim. The board's composition — former politicians, military officials, and diplomats — had no relevant expertise to evaluate whether the blood-testing technology worked. Holmes controlled information flow to the board and to investors. No independent scientific review was conducted before commercial partnerships were signed or patient tests were run. The board's function was reputational and network-oriented, not oversight-oriented.

## Key Decision Failures

- **Zero board members with relevant domain expertise**: A medical device company's board had no clinical laboratory scientists, biomedical engineers, or diagnostics industry veterans who could evaluate the core technology claim
- **No independent technical due diligence**: Walgreens and Safeway signed commercial partnerships without independent laboratory validation of Theranos's technology; both relied on Theranos's own demonstrations
- **Founder controlled all information to the board**: Holmes decided what the board saw; there was no mechanism for board members to interact directly with scientists, engineers, or regulators
- **NDAs weaponized against whistleblowers**: Employees who had concerns about test accuracy were threatened with litigation for speaking to journalists or regulators, eliminating the feedback mechanism that would have surfaced the problem
- **Prestige-based board selection**: Board members were chosen for their names and networks, not their oversight capability; their presence provided legitimacy that deterred external scrutiny

## Lessons

1. **Board composition must match what the board is overseeing**: A board without scientific expertise cannot evaluate scientific claims. At least one director per domain critical to the company's core value proposition should have the expertise to audit it.
2. **Independent technical diligence is not optional for novel science**: When a company's valuation rests on a technology claim that has not been externally validated, investors and commercial partners must commission independent validation — not accept the company's demonstrations.
3. **Information access is a governance mechanism**: If the board can only see what the CEO chooses to show them, it cannot function as an independent check. Board members should have direct access to technical leadership, auditors, and regulators.
4. **Prestige and oversight are different things**: Famous names on a board deter questions from outsiders; they do not substitute for the technical literacy or adversarial diligence that genuine oversight requires.
