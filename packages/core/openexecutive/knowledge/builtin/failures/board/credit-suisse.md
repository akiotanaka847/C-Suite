# Credit Suisse Collapse (2023)

## Situation

Credit Suisse was a 167-year-old Swiss bank, one of the 30 global systemically important financial institutions (G-SIBs) designated by regulators as too important to fail without endangering global financial stability. With $1.4 trillion in assets under management and operations in 50 countries, it was considered a pillar of global finance. By 2020 its investment bank had become one of the most profitable in Europe.

## What Happened

Between 2020 and 2023 Credit Suisse sustained a series of severe losses and reputational shocks. In 2021, it lost approximately $5.5 billion when Archegos Capital Management — a family office with significant undisclosed leverage — collapsed, and approximately $3 billion when supply chain finance firm Greensill Capital failed, taking Credit Suisse-sponsored funds with it. A spying scandal, in which the bank was found to have surveilled a former executive, forced the CEO to resign. A succession of replacement CEOs followed. Regulatory penalties mounted across multiple jurisdictions. In March 2023 — the same week as SVB — Credit Suisse disclosed material weaknesses in its financial reporting. Depositors and investors fled. The Swiss government brokered an emergency takeover by UBS in five days, eliminating $17 billion in Credit Suisse AT1 bonds and wiping out subordinated creditors.

## Root Cause

Credit Suisse's board and executive leadership failed repeatedly to instill or enforce a coherent risk culture. Each scandal was treated as a discrete event to be managed — litigation settled, executive replaced, apology issued — rather than as evidence of a systemic governance failure. The board did not act on the pattern. Risk appetite frameworks existed on paper; the investment bank operated as if they did not. Governance was further complicated by a historically adversarial relationship between the board and successive CEO tenures, with strategy reversals and restructuring announcements that undermined management credibility with markets.

## Key Decision Failures

- **Archegos exposure allowed to accumulate without disclosure**: Multiple prime brokerage desks extended large credit to Archegos without reconciling total exposure across the firm. A bank-wide risk view of client leverage was not maintained.
- **Pattern of scandals not treated as governance signal**: The board replaced individual executives after each scandal but never commissioned an independent root-cause review of why controls failed systemically. Each incident was treated as personnel, not culture.
- **Restructuring announced repeatedly without execution credibility**: Credit Suisse announced strategic restructurings in 2021, 2022, and 2023. Each failed to stabilize confidence. Markets stopped believing the announcements, which accelerated the loss of depositor confidence when the March 2023 crisis hit.
- **AT1 bond writedown handled without warning**: The Swiss regulator's decision to zero-out $17 billion of AT1 bonds while equity received some recovery inverted the normal creditor hierarchy. The decision destroyed trust in Swiss regulatory framework for a class of instruments globally.

## Lessons

1. **Boards must treat repeated misconduct as a risk culture failure, not an individual failure**: A single scandal can be an outlier. Two suggest a pattern. Three confirm a culture problem. The board's job is to recognize the pattern and act on the institution, not the person.
2. **Disclosed restructurings without operational evidence destroy credibility**: Markets will fund a struggling institution that shows credible operational progress. They will not fund one that announces restructurings and then reports further losses. Announcements without execution are worse than silence.
3. **Interconnected risk positions require firm-wide aggregation**: Archegos's leverage was visible to each desk individually and invisible to the institution as a whole. Concentration risk management requires a centralized view that no single business unit can obscure.
