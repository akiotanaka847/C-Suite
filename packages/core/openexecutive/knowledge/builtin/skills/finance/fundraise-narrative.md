# Fundraise Narrative

A fundraising narrative is the story you tell investors over and over for 6-12 weeks. The pitch deck and one-pager flow from it; the answers to investor questions flow from it; the post-close press flows from it. Getting the narrative right makes every other artifact easier.

## Inputs to gather first

Before drafting, confirm or ask for:

1. **The round being raised** — stage, target size, target valuation, use of proceeds
2. **Current traction** — ARR, growth rate, retention, key customers (with permission to name)
3. **The team** — founder backgrounds, key hires
4. **The strategic insight** — why this company, why now
5. **The competitive landscape** — direct competitors, alternatives, how you win
6. **The 18-month plan** — milestones the round funds you toward

If any of these is missing, stop and ask. A narrative without these inputs is a wish, not a pitch.

## The narrative arc — eight beats

A complete fundraise narrative covers eight beats, in this order:

### 1. The hook (1-2 sentences)

What's the one-line claim that makes the investor want to know more?

- "[Company] is building the [category] that [outcome] for [customer]"
- Concrete. Specific. Differentiated. No buzzwords.

Example: "Open Executive is the open-source AI executive that gives 200-person companies the strategic-thinking capability of a full C-suite at 5% of the cost."

### 2. The problem (1-2 paragraphs)

The specific pain in the customer's life. In their words. With a measurable cost.

- Who has the problem
- What it costs them today (in dollars, time, missed opportunity)
- Why existing solutions fail

Bad: "Executives are expensive."
Good: "A 200-person company's COO spends 60% of their time on tactical fires; the strategic work that should justify the $250k comp doesn't get done. Today, that company hires a $30k consultant for one quarterly project, then loses the institutional context the moment the engagement ends."

### 3. The why-now (1 paragraph)

What's changed in the world that makes this possible / inevitable now? Why doesn't a 2021 version of this company already exist and dominate?

Strong why-nows:
- Foundation models reached threshold capability for executive-level reasoning (2024)
- Cost of AI inference dropped 90% (last 18 months)
- Mid-market acceptance of AI-driven work tools (post-2023)

Weak why-nows:
- "AI is hot"
- "The market is growing"

### 4. The product (1-2 paragraphs)

What you've built. Concrete, not vision.

- The user experience (concrete, not abstract)
- The technical insight or capability that's unique
- Why this is hard for competitors to replicate

Include 1-2 specifics that prove you've built something real (a feature that took 6 months; a customer outcome).

### 5. The traction (the numbers)

The proof that the market wants what you've built.

For each stage:

- **Pre-seed**: design partners; LOIs; depth of customer engagement
- **Seed**: $0-1M ARR; conversion metrics; first reference customer
- **Series A**: $1-5M ARR; NRR >100%; CAC payback trending; first repeatable channel
- **Series B**: $5-15M ARR; NRR >110%; multi-channel; named brand customers
- **Series C**: $15M+ ARR; NRR >115%; scaling efficiently; clear path to $100M

Show traction with: ARR, growth rate, NRR/GRR, CAC payback, customer logos (with permission), magic number, burn multiple.

**The discipline**: 5-7 metrics that prove the business is working. More than that and they cancel each other out.

### 6. The market (TAM, with build-up)

Size of the opportunity, defended.

- TAM with bottoms-up build (see `market-sizing.md`)
- SAM (what you can address today)
- 3-year SOM (realistic capture)
- Adjacent expansion opportunities (where you grow next)

Avoid: "$50B market" with no math. Investors discount unsupported TAMs to zero.

### 7. The competition (clearly)

How you win.

- 2-3 named competitors and how you're different
- The alternatives (including doing nothing)
- Your unique position on the map

The honest "we have no competition" answer is almost always wrong — and even when right, makes investors worry the market doesn't exist.

### 8. The team (1 paragraph per founder, 1 line per key hire)

Why this team will win.

- Founders' relevant experience (concrete; not "20 years in tech")
- Key hires already on board
- The team gap the round funds

### 9. The ask (specific)

What you're raising and what you'll do with it.

- Round size and target valuation (range)
- Use of proceeds (% to engineering, % to GTM, % to ops)
- 18-month milestones (the specific outcomes the round funds)
- Next round timing and target metrics

## The artifacts

The narrative produces three artifacts:

**1. The one-pager** (a single page)
- Hook + problem + product + traction + team + ask
- Sent to investors as the first touch
- 200-400 words

**2. The pitch deck** (12-15 slides)
- Slide-per-beat structure
- Visual; minimal text
- Designed for live presentation, not for reading

**3. The data room** (after first meeting)
- Detailed financials and metrics
- Customer references and case studies
- Product demos and roadmap
- Team bios
- Cap table

## Discipline

- **The narrative comes before the deck.** Write the story first; design slides second.
- **One narrative, infinitely retold.** You'll tell this story 50+ times in a fundraise.
- **Tested with friendly investors first.** Get feedback before the A-list meetings.
- **Tightened with each rehearsal.** The 5th telling should be sharper than the 1st.
- **Adapted by audience, but anchored**. Same story; different emphasis for SaaS-focused vs. AI-focused investors.

## What NOT to include

- Vague claims ("massive market", "best team")
- Buzzwords disconnected from substance ("AI-powered", "next-gen")
- Defensive language ("though competitors exist, we...")
- "Eventually we'll..." features (only ship features you've shipped)
- Made-up TAMs
- Customer logos without permission
- Forward-looking financials beyond 18 months at most

## Closing summary

End the narrative draft with three sentences:

1. The hook (the one-line claim)
2. The most defensible piece of traction
3. The biggest risk the round addresses
