# Positioning Statement

A positioning statement is the most important paragraph the marketing team will ever write. It defines who you're for, what you compete against, and why customers should choose you. Every piece of marketing flows from it.

## Inputs to gather first

Before drafting anything, confirm or ask for:

1. **The product or segment being positioned** — if there are multiple products, which one
2. **The current customer base** — who's actually buying; who's NOT buying
3. **The competitive alternatives** — what would customers use if you didn't exist (including manual, in-house, doing nothing)
4. **The product's unique attributes** — what does it do that the alternatives don't
5. **Recent win/loss interviews** — what's actually said in deal cycles
6. **The company's strategic direction** — where you're heading, not just where you are

If any of these is missing, stop and ask. Positioning written without customer voice and competitive context produces a corporate marketing statement, not real positioning.

## The five inputs (April Dunford framework)

Work through these in order. The positioning statement assembles from them.

### 1. Competitive alternatives

What would your customers use if you didn't exist? Be honest and broad:
- Direct competitors (other vendors in the category)
- Adjacent vendors (different but solves a similar problem)
- DIY / internal builds
- Manual processes (spreadsheets, person-power)
- Status quo (doing nothing)

For each, note WHY a customer would choose it. This frames what you need to beat.

### 2. Unique attributes

What does your product have that the alternatives don't? Be specific:
- Features (not generic — what specifically)
- Capabilities (not just "AI" — what AI does)
- Architecture or technical approach
- Business model or pricing
- Ecosystem (integrations, partnerships, community)

A unique attribute is one that competitors can't easily replicate or won't.

### 3. Value (benefits)

For each unique attribute, what value does it produce for the customer?

The chain:
- Attribute → Capability → Benefit → Value

Example:
- *Attribute*: open-source self-hosted deployment
- *Capability*: data stays in customer's infrastructure
- *Benefit*: meets enterprise security and data sovereignty requirements
- *Value*: enables enterprise adoption that competitors can't serve

Value is what the customer is buying. Attributes are the proof.

### 4. Best-fit customer characteristics

For the value you provide, which customers value it MOST?

Identify:
- Firmographics: industry, company size, geography
- Situation: growth stage, technology stack, regulatory environment
- Pain: what specific problem they have acutely
- Buyer: who within the company makes this decision; what they care about

The best-fit customer isn't your TAM. It's the customer for whom your unique value is most acute.

### 5. Market category

The frame of reference customers use to understand and evaluate you.

- An existing category (the easier path — customer knows what it is, has budget for it)
- A new category (the harder path — requires education but enables premium positioning)

Pick deliberately. Most companies under-think this.

## Output structure

### The positioning statement (one paragraph, ~50-80 words)

A template you fill in:

> For **[best-fit customer]**
> Who has **[problem]**
> **[Product]** is a **[market category]**
> That **[unique value]**
> Unlike **[competitive alternatives]**
> Because **[unique attributes and capabilities]**

Example:

> For 200-person B2B SaaS companies that have outgrown founder-led strategy but can't yet afford a full CFO and CSO, Open Executive is an AI executive that delivers board-level strategic analysis on demand. Unlike traditional consultancies that cost $50k+ per engagement and lose context after delivery, Open Executive is open-source, self-hosted, and continuously learns from your company's data — giving every weekly question the depth of a $250k senior advisor.

### The three pillars (supporting claims)

From the positioning, derive 3 pillars — the value propositions that show up on the website, sales deck, product page.

Each pillar:
- One short headline (3-5 words)
- One sentence explaining
- 2-3 proof points (features, customer outcomes, metrics)

Example:
- **Open-source and self-hosted** — your strategic data never leaves your infrastructure
- **Continuously contextual** — every conversation builds on your company's actual data
- **Fractional cost** — replaces $30k+ engagements with subscription pricing

### The anti-positioning (who you're NOT for)

Explicitly state the customers you don't serve well. This is a quality signal — it tells the right customers they're in the right place, and the wrong customers to look elsewhere.

Example:
- Not for: enterprises requiring SOC 2 Type II certification (today)
- Not for: companies wanting purely conversational AI without strategic structure
- Not for: industries with regulated AI requirements (healthcare, certain financial services)

## Discipline

- **Customer language, not internal language.** What did the latest 5 buyers actually say?
- **Specific, not generic.** "Strategy" vs. "AI executive that runs your quarterly planning."
- **Defensible against the alternatives.** If a competitor said "we do that too," your positioning has failed.
- **Tested before published.** Show it to 3 customers, 3 prospects, 3 internal team members. Note where it lands and where it doesn't.
- **Single source of truth.** Once finalized, every team uses it. Marketing, sales, product, support, hiring — all draw from the same well.

## Validation tests

For your draft, ask:
1. **The customer test**: would a target customer recognize themselves and say "yes, that's me, and yes, that's my problem"?
2. **The competitor test**: would a sales rep be able to use this to differentiate from named competitors?
3. **The product test**: does the product actually deliver on the value claims?
4. **The "would they argue?" test**: could a competitor credibly claim the opposite of any of this? If not, you're saying platitudes.
5. **The internal test**: do 3 separate people in the company independently describe the company the same way?

If any test fails, revise.

## What NOT to include

- Buzzwords ("AI-powered", "next-generation", "innovative") — describes everyone, distinguishes no one
- Internal jargon ("we have a unique architecture") — customers don't care
- Lists of features (positioning is about the customer, not the product)
- Generic value props ("save time and money") — true of everything
- Comparison framing that's actually weak ("we have similar features but better UX") — implies you're a me-too

## Closing summary

End the positioning work with three things:

1. The one-line positioning ("we are X for Y who needs Z")
2. The 3 pillars
3. The anti-positioning (who we're NOT for)

These three artifacts, applied consistently, are the foundation of everything customer-facing.
