# IPO and Late-Stage Financial Readiness

The financial work of becoming a public company takes 18-36 months. Companies that start late either miss their window or go public with embarrassing post-IPO restatements. The work below applies whether the actual outcome is an IPO, a strategic acquisition, a direct listing, or a SPAC.

## The Two Question Test

Before any IPO conversation seriously starts, the company should pass two tests:

**1. Are we predictable?**
- Forecast accuracy of ±5% on revenue, ±10% on EBITDA, for 6+ quarters running
- Public markets punish surprises (negative MORE than positive)
- If forecasts are wrong, the underlying business isn't ready, regardless of size

**2. Are we durable?**
- Net Revenue Retention >115% (SaaS) — proves the business compounds
- Gross margins in the right band for the category
- Customer concentration <10% from any single customer
- Multiple growth vectors (not single-channel dependent)
- The Rule of 40 (growth rate + EBITDA margin) at 40+ for SaaS

A company that's $200M ARR but unpredictable and concentrated is not IPO-ready. A company that's $100M ARR, predictable, and durable might be.

## The Public Company Financial Stack

Going public requires standing up financial infrastructure that most private companies don't have.

**Accounting:**
- GAAP financials, audited annually
- Quarterly review (not full audit) by the same firm
- Internal accounting team capable of monthly close in 5-10 business days
- Big 4 or similar audit firm relationship — switch from regional firm 18-24 months pre-IPO

**Controls (SOX 404):**
- Documented internal controls over financial reporting
- Tested annually by management and auditors
- Material weaknesses in controls = disclosure obligation = stock-price hit
- Begin work 18-24 months pre-IPO; SOX-ready usually requires a dedicated Controls Manager and an internal audit function

**Revenue recognition:**
- ASC 606 compliance for revenue (rules-based but interpretation-heavy)
- Many SaaS companies have hidden rev rec issues (multi-year deals, performance obligations, usage components)
- Address pre-IPO — restatements post-IPO are catastrophic

**FP&A discipline:**
- Quarterly forecast within ±5% of actuals
- Annual guidance to The Street within achievable range
- Investor-grade KPI definitions, applied consistently across quarters

**Equity administration:**
- Cap table cleanliness — every grant, transfer, exercise documented
- 409A valuations current and defensible
- Stock plan that scales to public-company needs

## The Working Capital Story

Public investors scrutinize working capital intensely.

**Deferred revenue:**
- For subscription businesses, deferred revenue is a positive working-capital signal (customers prepay)
- Growing deferred revenue indicates a strengthening business
- Disclose customer prepayment terms clearly

**Days Sales Outstanding (DSO):**
- Public companies disclose DSO trends
- Rising DSO = collection problems or aggressive revenue recognition
- Target steady DSO; trends speak louder than absolute values

**Days Payables Outstanding (DPO):**
- Stretching payables to fund growth shows up in working capital metrics
- Aggressive payables timing can mask underlying cash issues

**Free Cash Flow:**
- The metric public investors most often watch (for SaaS, frequently more than EBITDA)
- Definition: Operating Cash Flow - Maintenance Capex
- Trend matters more than absolute value early; positive FCF inflection is the milestone

## The S-1 / Prospectus

The public filing that introduces the company. It's a legal document AND a marketing document.

**Standard sections:**
- Prospectus summary (the elevator pitch)
- Risk factors (lawyer-driven; long and scary; market expects this)
- Market opportunity (TAM, SAM, SOM — substantiated)
- Business description (strategy, products, GTM)
- Management discussion & analysis (MD&A) of financials
- Financial statements (3 years audited; latest quarterly)
- Management and ownership
- Underwriting and use of proceeds

**The financial disclosures that matter most:**
- Revenue by segment / product / geography
- Customer concentration disclosure
- Cohort retention curves
- Non-GAAP reconciliations (adjusted EBITDA, free cash flow)
- Stock-based compensation expense and share counts (post-IPO)

**The S-1 is filed initially confidentially** (since JOBS Act), allowing back-and-forth with SEC before public visibility. Public filing happens 2-3 weeks before the roadshow.

## The Roadshow

Two-week marketing tour to institutional investors. The CEO + CFO present 8-12 times per day.

**The presentation:**
- 30-40 minute pitch (lifted from the S-1, made more investor-friendly)
- 20-30 minute Q&A
- Same pitch repeated to different audiences

**Bookbuilding:**
- Underwriters collect indicative orders from investors
- Final pricing set based on demand
- Pricing too low = "leaving money on the table" but happy first-day investors
- Pricing too high = poor first-day trading + sticky negative reputation

**The first day:**
- Underwriters price the evening before
- Stock opens for trading; first-day "pop" of 10-20% is now common (and increasingly criticized as transfer to institutional investors)
- The CEO's first 90 days as a public-company CEO begin

## Post-IPO Realities

The work doesn't end at the IPO. In some ways, it begins.

**Quarterly earnings cycle:**
- Earnings release + 10-Q filing within ~45 days of quarter end
- Earnings call (60-90 min) with analyst Q&A
- Guidance (or no-guidance) for the upcoming period
- A miss against guidance can cut stock 10-30% in a day

**Analyst coverage:**
- 8-15 sell-side analysts will eventually cover the stock
- Each builds their own model; consensus estimates form
- Analyst day, often annual, to set strategic narrative

**Lock-up:**
- 180-day lock-up on pre-IPO shares; employees can't sell
- End of lock-up = massive volume increase; often pressures stock
- Some companies do staged lock-up releases to manage this

**Insider trading discipline:**
- Material non-public information (MNPI) restrictions
- Blackout windows around quarterly earnings
- 10b5-1 plans for executives to systematically sell stock without violating insider rules

**Stock-based compensation expense:**
- Dilutes existing shareholders; appears on income statement
- Public investors increasingly focus on SBC as a real cost
- Net dilution targets (typically <2% per year) become a thing to manage

## SPAC and Direct Listing Alternatives

**SPAC (Special Purpose Acquisition Company):**
- Faster path to public (3-6 months vs. 18-36 for traditional IPO)
- Allows forward-looking projections in the S-4 (vs. backward-only S-1)
- Generally results in lower valuations and worse trading post-listing
- Most 2021-2022 SPAC mergers have traded poorly; current investor skepticism is high

**Direct listing:**
- No new shares issued; existing shareholders sell directly to public
- No bookbuilding; market-determined price discovery
- Works for companies with brand recognition and no immediate capital need
- Examples: Spotify, Slack, Coinbase

**Standard IPO is still the dominant path** for most growth-stage tech companies. The alternatives are situational.

## When NOT to IPO

The decision to go public is not just "are we ready" — it's "is this the right path?"

**Reasons to delay or skip an IPO:**
- Strong private capital available at attractive terms
- Public market multiples currently compressed for your category
- Strategic acquirer interest (often pays higher than public market valuation)
- Volatility in your numbers that would punish you publicly
- CEO / leadership team's appetite for public-company life (it's relentless)

**The trend:** companies stay private longer than they used to. $1B+ "unicorns" can stay private indefinitely with secondaries providing employee liquidity.

## The IPO Readiness Diagnostic

For your company, honestly answer:
1. What's our 8-quarter forecast accuracy on revenue? On EBITDA?
2. Can our internal team close the books in 10 business days?
3. When was our last SOC 2 Type II? When did our auditor last find a material issue?
4. Is our cap table clean? Every grant documented? 409A current?
5. If we filed an S-1 tomorrow, what's the disclosed risk that would tank the stock first day?

If any answer is uncertain, the IPO is more than 18 months away — regardless of revenue.
