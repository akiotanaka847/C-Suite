# Fundraising for Technology Companies

## When to Raise

Raise when:
- You have clear evidence of product-market fit (NRR > 100%, organic growth, declining CAC)
- You have a specific use of proceeds that will create measurable value
- Your runway is 9-12 months (not 3 months — desperation is visible to investors)
- The market window favors your category

Don't raise just because you can. Venture capital is expensive money. Every dollar of VC you take costs you equity and creates pressure for a large outcome.

## Round Sizing

**Rule**: Raise enough to hit the milestones that will make the next round compelling, plus 6 months buffer.

Typical milestones by stage:
- **Pre-Seed → Seed**: Prove the product works for a real customer with a real problem
- **Seed → Series A**: $1-3M ARR, evidence of repeatable sales motion, NRR > 100%
- **Series A → Series B**: $5-15M ARR, unit economics within range, clear path to $50M+ ARR
- **Series B → Series C**: $20-50M ARR, efficient growth (Rule of 40 > 40), market leadership signal

Raise 18-24 months of runway at current burn (not projected — current).

## Valuation Frameworks

**Revenue multiple**: Most common for SaaS. Current market multiples vary widely with interest rates and sentiment. As a baseline: high-growth (80%+) SaaS at Series A commands 10-15x forward ARR; moderate-growth (50-80%) commands 6-10x.

**Comparable transactions**: Pull 3-5 recent funding announcements in your category at your stage. They're publicly disclosed. Adjust for your growth rate, NRR, and gross margin.

**The negotiation**: Valuation is a negotiation. Your BATNA (best alternative) determines your leverage. Multiple term sheets dramatically improve your position.

## Term Sheet Essentials

**Economics terms** (these determine your outcome):
- Pre-money valuation: the value of the company before the investment
- Investment amount → post-money = pre + investment
- Option pool: usually created pre-money (dilutes you, not the investor). Push for post-money.
- Liquidation preference: 1x non-participating is market. 2x or participating preferred is investor-friendly and should be resisted.
- Anti-dilution: broad-based weighted average is market. Full ratchet is very investor-friendly; avoid it.
- Pro rata rights: investor's right to participate in future rounds. Reasonable to grant.

**Control terms** (these determine who runs the company):
- Board seats: investors typically get 1 seat per round. At Series A, typical is 2 founders, 1 investor, 2 independents.
- Protective provisions: investors veto certain actions (selling company, issuing new stock, etc.). Standard and non-negotiable, but scope matters — review carefully.
- Information rights: standard. Monthly financials, annual budget.
- Drag-along rights: can force minority shareholders to approve a sale. Standard.

**The most important term**: Liquidation preference. In a downside scenario (acqui-hire or below-value acquisition), this determines whether founders and employees get anything. 1x non-participating is fine. 1x participating means investors get their money back first, then participate in the remaining proceeds. On a $50M exit with $30M raised at 1x participating: investors get $30M + share of remaining $20M. Founders/employees share the rest.

## Investor Diligence Checklist

Before a term sheet, investors will diligence:
- Financial model and historical financials
- Cap table
- Customer references (3-5 customers they'll call without you present)
- Team backgrounds (they will check references you didn't give them)
- Product demo
- Pipeline and sales process
- Legal diligence (IP ownership, employment agreements, past litigation)

Prepare these in a data room before starting the process. Deal velocity matters.

## Managing the Process

Run a competitive process:
1. Identify 15-20 investors (tiered: A-list, B-list, exploratory)
2. Take exploratory meetings with B-list first to refine your pitch
3. Run A-list meetings in a compressed 2-3 week window
4. Create artificial scarcity: "We're hoping to close in 30 days"
5. First term sheet triggers others — investors hate missing deals

The moment you have a term sheet, tell every investor you're in conversations with. You're not legally required to disclose the term, but signaling competition is fair game.
