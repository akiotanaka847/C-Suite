# Capital Allocation

Capital allocation is how a leadership team translates strategy into where the money actually goes. It is one of the most consequential decisions a CEO and CFO make repeatedly — and one of the least disciplined in most companies.

## The Five Capital Allocation Choices

For any company with cash on the balance sheet or cash being generated, there are exactly five things you can do with it. Every dollar goes somewhere on this list.

**1. Reinvest in the core business**
- Hire more people, build more product, expand sales coverage
- Highest expected return for high-growth businesses with PMF
- The default for most growth-stage tech companies

**2. Reinvest in new businesses / adjacencies**
- Build new product lines, enter new geographies, fund H2/H3 bets
- Lower confidence than core; higher long-term upside
- Should be a deliberate portfolio, not orphan projects

**3. Acquire**
- Buy capability, talent, or revenue you can't build fast enough
- Capital-intensive; integration risk; 60-70% of deals destroy value
- Reserved for specific strategic gaps

**4. Pay down debt**
- Reduces interest expense and balance-sheet risk
- Right call when debt is expensive relative to your investment opportunities
- Underused by tech companies who treat debt as free money during low-rate periods

**5. Return to shareholders**
- Dividends or share buybacks
- Default for mature businesses with limited reinvestment opportunities
- Rare for growth-stage tech; common for late-stage / public

The discipline: rank the next dollar's return across all five options. Allocate to the highest-return option that has remaining capacity.

## The Hurdle Rate

Before deploying capital to any investment, the return should clear a hurdle rate — the minimum acceptable return given the risk.

**Common hurdle rates:**
- Cost of capital (WACC): the lowest possible bar
- Cost of equity: more relevant for equity-funded businesses
- Target return: often 2-3x WACC, especially for early-stage / high-risk investments

**For a growth-stage SaaS:**
- WACC: typically 15-20% (equity-funded; high implied cost)
- Hurdle rate for incremental investments: often 25-30%+ to clear the bar of just continuing to scale the core

**The honest test:**
- If we deployed this dollar into our existing motion (more sales reps, more marketing), what's the return?
- If the answer is 40% IRR and the proposed investment is 25% IRR, the answer is "no" — keep funding the core

## ROIC — The Truth-Telling Metric

Return on Invested Capital = After-tax operating profit / Invested capital

It tells you whether your business is creating economic value (ROIC > cost of capital) or destroying it (ROIC < cost of capital).

For SaaS / pre-profitability businesses, adapt:
- *Customer-level ROIC*: lifetime contribution margin / CAC. Should clear hurdle by year 3.
- *Cohort-level ROIC*: total cash invested in a cohort / lifetime cash returned

**The pattern to watch:** ROIC declining as you scale. Common cause: each marginal customer is more expensive to acquire and contributes less. Means the business is becoming less efficient even if absolute growth continues.

## Capital Allocation by Stage

**Pre-PMF / pre-Seed:**
- 95% in the core (product + minimal go-to-market for learning)
- Capital allocation discipline: low. Survival and learning matter more.

**Seed / Series A — finding the motion:**
- 70% core (product + initial GTM)
- 20% scaling the channels that are working
- 10% experimentation (next channel, next segment)

**Series B / C — scaling what works:**
- 60% core scaling (proven channels, proven segments, more people)
- 20% adjacent (new segments, new products in proven category)
- 10-15% strategic (M&A, geographic expansion)
- 5-10% optionality (R&D bets, new categories)

**Late stage / pre-IPO:**
- 50% core
- 25% adjacent
- 15% strategic / M&A
- 10% optionality + balance sheet (cash, debt repayment)

**Public / mature:**
- Higher percentage to shareholders (dividends, buybacks) as growth opportunities saturate
- Strategic acquisitions become more important
- Some capital often allocated to large transformation bets

## The Annual Planning Decision

The annual budget is the most consequential capital allocation moment. Discipline:

**1. Start from zero, not last year**
- Last year's allocations reflect last year's reality
- Force re-justification of each line
- (Zero-based budgeting is exhausting if literal; modified approach — re-examine the top 5-10 lines and accept the others — works)

**2. Allocate by outcome, not function**
- "$5M to demand generation" is a function
- "$5M to expand into mid-market segment" is an outcome
- The outcome view forces explicit choices about where the dollar moves the strategy

**3. Force trade-offs**
- If every function wants 30% more headcount, who's prioritizing?
- Force net-zero choices: "to add headcount in X, what comes out elsewhere?"

**4. Sequence the spending**
- A 10% headcount add Q1 vs. Q3 has very different cash and outcome profiles
- Plan the cadence, not just the totals

**5. Define unlock criteria for second-half spending**
- "We'll add the Q3 sales hires IF Q2 quota attainment is >80%"
- Pre-committing to triggers prevents reactive cuts or overspends

## Reallocation — The Most Underused Lever

The best capital allocators don't just allocate once a year. They reallocate continuously.

**Signals to reallocate:**
- A channel that was producing has degraded (cut spend, reallocate to better channel)
- A team that was over-resourced for a project is now done (reallocate headcount)
- A segment that looked promising isn't materializing (kill investment, refocus)
- A new opportunity emerged mid-year (find the dollars to fund it)

**Why companies underreallocate:**
- Politics: cutting a function admits the original allocation was wrong
- Sunk-cost thinking: "we've already spent $X on it"
- No reallocation mechanism: no quarterly review with explicit authority to cut and shift

**The mechanism:**
- Quarterly capital allocation review with CFO + CEO + functional VPs
- Explicit list of investments performing vs. underperforming
- Authority to shift 10-15% of allocations between functions without re-opening annual plan

## M&A as Capital Allocation

Acquisitions are a capital allocation choice that competes with reinvestment.

**The honest test before any deal:**
- If we deployed the acquisition cost into our existing motion, what's the return?
- The acquisition should clear that return PLUS the integration risk and execution cost
- Most deals don't pass this test honestly — they pass it through optimistic synergy assumptions

**Capital efficiency of M&A vs. organic:**
- Acquisitions front-load cost; organic builds amortize over time
- Acquisitions buy speed; organic builds tend to be cheaper per dollar of capability
- Best for: capability gaps that can't be built fast enough; talent in scarce labor markets; consolidation in roll-up markets

## Common Capital Allocation Failures

**1. Allocating by squeaky wheel**
- Loudest VP gets the most headcount
- Strategic priority becomes irrelevant
- *Cure*: top-down allocation by strategic priority, then bottoms-up build within the envelope

**2. Cutting the wrong things in downturns**
- R&D and marketing are easiest to cut, hardest to restart
- Sales cuts hit revenue fastest
- Best practice: protect demand gen and core R&D; trim G&A and discretionary first
- Worst practice: across-the-board "everyone cut 15%" — penalizes high-performers, rewards bloat

**3. Hoarding cash**
- Conservative CFOs sometimes sit on cash beyond what's prudent
- Excess cash earns sub-WACC returns; opportunity cost is real
- *Rule of thumb*: maintain 18-24 months of runway in cash; excess should be deployed or planned

**4. Continuous incremental adds**
- Every quarter, +3 headcount here, +5 marketing dollars there
- No moment of explicit choice; bloat accumulates
- *Cure*: explicit semi-annual reset with hard envelopes

**5. Not killing failed investments**
- A failed product, a flat-lining geo, an underperforming channel — left to limp along
- Cumulative cost is enormous; alternative use of the same capital would have produced more
- *Cure*: each major investment has explicit kill criteria; review against them quarterly

## The Capital Allocation Diagnostic

For your current spending, ask:
1. Top 3 places we're investing — what's the expected ROIC?
2. Top 3 places we should be investing but aren't — why not?
3. What did we kill (or trim materially) in the last 12 months?
4. If we suddenly had 20% more capital, where would it go?
5. If we suddenly had 20% less, where would it come from?

If answers 4 and 5 aren't symmetric (i.e., you'd add somewhere different than you'd cut), something is off in current allocation — there's a priority you'd fund with new money that you're not protecting with existing money.
