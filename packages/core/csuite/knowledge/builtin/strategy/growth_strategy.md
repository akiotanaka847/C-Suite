# Growth Strategy

Growth isn't a single lever. It's a portfolio of bets — some compounding slowly, some delivering immediately, some failing entirely. The discipline is choosing which bets, sequencing them, and reading the signals fast enough to double down or kill them.

## The Growth Equation

Most B2B businesses grow through some combination of:

```
Growth = (New Customer Acquisition) + (Expansion within Customers) − (Churn)
```

The three levers behave differently:

- **Acquisition** — capital-intensive, externally-paced (channels, market), scales with spend up to channel ceilings
- **Expansion** — capital-light, internally-paced, scales with customer success motion and product depth
- **Churn reduction** — defensive, compounds over time, often the highest-ROI move you're not making

A useful framing: for every dollar of CAC you spend, how much expansion ARR does the resulting customer cohort generate over 3 years? In best-in-class SaaS, expansion ARR exceeds initial ACV within 24-36 months.

## The Three Horizons (McKinsey)

Time-phased view of growth investment:

**Horizon 1 — Defend and extend the core (12 months)**
- The existing business, growing in current segments with current products
- Highest predictability; funds the rest
- Typical investment: 70% of growth spend

**Horizon 2 — Build emerging businesses (12-36 months)**
- Adjacent expansion: new segments, new products, new geographies
- Higher uncertainty; clear thesis; measurable milestones
- Typical investment: 20% of growth spend

**Horizon 3 — Create options for the future (36+ months)**
- Genuinely new businesses: new categories, transformational bets
- High uncertainty; long payback; portfolio approach (most won't work)
- Typical investment: 10% of growth spend

**The trap:** companies under-invest in H2 and H3 during good times (because H1 is plenty) and over-cut H2/H3 in downturns (because returns are far off). The result is a brittle business with no future engine.

## The Growth Loop

A growth loop is a self-reinforcing cycle where output becomes input for the next iteration. Different from funnels (which have an end).

**Examples:**
- *Content-led*: content attracts users → users create content → content attracts more users (Stack Overflow, Reddit)
- *Network-led*: more users → more value per user → more users (Slack, LinkedIn)
- *Data-led*: more users → more data → better product → more users (Spotify recommendations, Netflix)
- *Marketplace*: more sellers → more buyers → more sellers (Amazon, Airbnb)
- *Referral*: paying customer refers another paying customer (Dropbox, Robinhood)

**Why loops beat funnels:**
- Funnels require constant top-of-funnel investment to keep flowing
- Loops compound — each cycle adds energy to the next
- A weak funnel always needs more spend; a working loop needs less over time

**The work:**
- Identify the loop your business COULD run on
- Instrument the loop metrics (loop velocity, loop conversion rate)
- Invest in the slowest part of the loop, not the easiest
- Most "growth experiments" are funnel optimizations; the highest-leverage ones are loop unlocks

## Channel Strategy

Most businesses over-rely on 1-2 channels. The discipline is to develop a portfolio that doesn't depend on any single channel staying healthy.

**Channel categories:**
- *Paid acquisition*: SEM, paid social, display, affiliate, paid podcast
- *Organic content*: SEO, social organic, podcasts, YouTube, communities
- *Outbound*: SDR-led, founder-led, partner-driven
- *Product-led*: virality, free tier, in-product growth
- *Partnerships*: integrations, co-marketing, channel resellers
- *Events*: conferences, dinners, webinars, owned events
- *PR*: earned media, thought leadership, analyst relations

**The 3-channel rule:**
- At least 3 channels each producing >15% of new ARR
- No single channel producing >50% of new ARR
- New channels in development pipeline so the portfolio refreshes

Companies that get 80% of new ARR from one channel are one algorithm change or competitor entry away from a growth cliff.

## Market Expansion Decisions

**Vertical expansion** — moving deeper in existing segments
- Pros: leverages existing brand and sales motion; higher win rates; faster sales cycles
- Cons: bounded by segment size; saturation eventually
- When: ICP is <30% saturated; expansion within ICP has higher LTV than new ICPs

**Adjacent vertical expansion** — entering a similar but distinct segment
- Pros: leverages product and learnings; reasonable extension
- Cons: requires segment-specific positioning, references, and often features
- When: original segment is becoming saturated; adjacent segments share buying patterns

**Geographic expansion** — entering new countries or regions
- Pros: large addressable market; sometimes less competition
- Cons: localization, regulation, sales infrastructure, support, languages — expensive and slow
- When: domestic market saturating; multiple anchor customers requesting; product is already proven domestically

**Upmarket** — moving from SMB toward enterprise
- Pros: larger ACVs, lower churn, better unit economics at scale
- Cons: longer sales cycles, more product requirements (SOC 2, SSO, advanced admin), enterprise sales motion
- When: SMB CAC has risen; product needs enterprise features anyway for any meaningful business

**Downmarket** — moving from enterprise toward SMB
- Pros: larger TAM; faster sales; product-led motion possible
- Cons: lower ACVs, higher churn, requires self-service product
- When: enterprise market is saturated; product can be simplified

**Choosing direction:** the right expansion vector depends on where the bigger margin pool is, where the team has competence, and where competitive dynamics are most favorable. Avoid expanding into two vectors at once — focus.

## Growth Anti-Patterns

**1. Throwing money at paid before PMF**
- Spending more before the product holds users; you accelerate the proof that PMF is missing
- The CAC will keep rising; LTV won't keep up

**2. The "we need a CMO" fallacy**
- Hiring a senior marketing leader to fix growth before the strategy is clear
- The CMO arrives, can't fix what isn't defined, leaves in 18 months
- Cure: define what good marketing looks like before the hire, not after

**3. Channel monomania**
- Doubling down on the channel that works "because it's working"
- Misses portfolio risk; misses diminishing returns until they hit
- Cure: develop the next channel BEFORE you need it

**4. The reorg for growth**
- Restructuring marketing/sales/CS as the answer to flat growth
- Solves coordination issues but rarely solves the underlying problem (positioning, ICP fit, product gap)
- Cure: diagnose first; if the diagnosis is "structure," then reorg

**5. Vanity expansion**
- "We launched in Europe" with 2 customers and 1 employee
- Press release wins; operational hangover for years
- Cure: launch sized to support, or don't launch

## Growth Diagnostic

Each quarter, ask:
1. What's our growth equation? Which lever moved most this quarter? Which moved least?
2. Are we running a loop or a funnel? Where's the slowest step?
3. What % of new ARR came from our top channel? Top 3?
4. What's our next expansion vector and why?
5. What growth investment did we kill this quarter? (If nothing, we're not pruning enough)

A growth strategy without explicit cuts is a wish list.
