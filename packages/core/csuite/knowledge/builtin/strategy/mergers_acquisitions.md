# Mergers, Acquisitions, and Corporate Development

## Build / Buy / Partner Decision

Before considering M&A, force the question through this filter:

- **Build** when: the capability is core to your differentiation, you have the talent, and time-to-market isn't fatal.
- **Buy** when: the capability is core but not yet built, the gap is more than 12 months, and the target has unique IP/talent/customers you can't replicate.
- **Partner** when: the capability is adjacent (not core), the partner has clearer comparative advantage, and you can get the same outcome without the integration cost.

The default bias should be Build, then Partner, then Buy. Acquisitions look like shortcuts and are not. 60-70% of acquisitions destroy shareholder value, mostly through over-payment, integration failure, or culture clash.

## Acquisition Types and Their Real Purpose

**Acqui-hire** — buying a small company primarily for its team. Price paid per engineer (typically $1-3M). Typically used when hiring through normal channels is too slow. Watch: most acqui-hired teams leave within 18-24 months of close. Build a retention package into the deal.

**Tuck-in** — bolt-on acquisition of a complementary product or capability. Integrated into existing business unit. Lower risk, often value-additive when priced right.

**Strategic** — significant capability or market expansion. Salesforce buying Slack, Microsoft buying LinkedIn. Driven by strategic thesis, not financial engineering. High integration cost, high upside if thesis holds.

**Platform** — buying a company that becomes a new business line or platform. Higher capital requirement, longer payback, larger transformation.

**Roll-up** — consolidating a fragmented industry through repeated acquisitions. Common in services, healthcare, dental practices, vertical SaaS. Value comes from scale, professional management, and multiple arbitrage (buying small at low multiples, selling consolidated at higher multiples).

## Deal Process

**1. Sourcing** (months)
- Build a target list against your strategic thesis
- Maintain "always-on" relationships with potential targets — the best deals happen when a CEO calls you, not when a banker calls them

**2. Initial outreach and LOI** (weeks)
- Indicative valuation range
- Headline structure (cash, stock, earnout)
- Exclusivity (typically 30-60 days)
- Conditions to close

**3. Due diligence** (4-12 weeks)
- Financial: historical financials, quality of earnings, customer concentration, working capital, debt
- Commercial: market validation, customer references, pipeline
- Legal: corporate structure, IP ownership, contracts, litigation, regulatory
- Tax: structure optimization, NOLs, transfer pricing
- HR: employment agreements, comp, benefits, retention risk
- Technology: code quality, technical debt, security, infrastructure

**4. Definitive agreements** (4-8 weeks)
- Stock vs. asset purchase
- Reps and warranties (and rep & warranty insurance — increasingly standard)
- Indemnification (caps, baskets, survival periods)
- Working capital adjustment
- Earnouts (if any) — define metrics precisely and watch for gaming

**5. Closing** — sign and close, or sign with closing conditions (regulatory, financing, third-party consents)

**6. Integration** — see below.

## Valuation Approaches in M&A

- **DCF** — discount the target's future cash flows. Useful for cash-generative businesses with predictable trajectories.
- **Comparable companies** — apply trading multiples (EV/Revenue, EV/EBITDA, P/E) of similar public companies. Adjust for size, growth, profitability differences.
- **Precedent transactions** — apply multiples paid in recent comparable acquisitions. Includes a control premium (typically 20-40% over trading prices).
- **Synergy value** — incremental cash flows from cost takeout or revenue acceleration. Discount aggressively — synergies are notoriously over-estimated.

Maximum bid = standalone value + share of expected synergies (typically 40-60% goes to the seller as the control premium).

## Integration — Where Deals Succeed or Fail

The deal is won or lost in the first 100 days post-close.

**Day 1 priorities:**
- Communicate the strategic rationale to BOTH companies' employees — same message, same day
- Name the integration leader (full-time, senior, with authority — not a side-of-desk role)
- Identify the top 10 customers/partners who need to hear from leadership personally that week

**First 100 days:**
- Decide which operating model (absorb, federate, hybrid) BEFORE close — don't punt this
- Identify the 5-10 key talent in the target and have explicit retention conversations (compensation, role, autonomy)
- Lock in quick wins that prove the deal thesis to both employee bases

**Synergy capture:**
- Cost synergies: typically achievable, but consume management bandwidth. Most are realized in 12-18 months.
- Revenue synergies: typically over-promised, under-delivered. Discount by 50%+ when modeling deal returns.

## Common Failure Modes

1. **Overpaying** — winning the auction and losing the deal economics. Stick to your walk-away price.
2. **Cultural collision** — incompatible operating models that no integration plan can bridge. Diagnose during diligence, not after close.
3. **Customer attrition** — overlapping accounts, account team changes, and uncertainty cause customers to take meetings with your competitors. Plan customer comms before announcement.
4. **Talent flight** — the people you bought leave, taking knowledge with them. Retention packages and early role clarity matter more than the cash price.
5. **Integration fatigue** — pursuing multiple acquisitions in parallel without digesting each. Most companies can integrate one strategic deal per 12-18 months.
