# Corporate Strategy Frameworks

Corporate strategy answers: which businesses should we be in, and how do they reinforce each other? Distinct from business unit strategy, which answers: how do we win in this specific market?

## Porter's Generic Strategies

Three sustainable positions. Companies that try to do all three usually do none of them well ("stuck in the middle").

**1. Cost Leadership** — be the lowest-cost producer in the industry. Profit from volume, scale, and operational excellence. Requires: scale, process discipline, no-frills positioning. Risks: imitation by larger players, technology shifts that reset the cost curve.

**2. Differentiation** — offer something the market values that competitors can't easily replicate. Premium pricing funds reinvestment in the differentiator. Requires: deep customer insight, R&D, brand. Risks: competitors close the gap; customer needs shift.

**3. Focus** — serve a narrow segment (geographic, demographic, vertical) better than generalists can. Cost-focus or differentiation-focus. Requires: deep segment expertise. Risks: segment becomes large enough that generalists notice.

Pick one and align every operational decision to it. The strategy is not the picked position — it's the thousand consistent choices that result from it.

## Ansoff Growth Matrix

Four growth strategies, ranked by risk:

|  | Existing Products | New Products |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing Markets** | Market Penetration (low risk) | Product Development (medium) |
| **New Markets** | Market Development (medium) | Diversification (high risk) |

- **Market Penetration**: sell more of what you have to who you have. Pricing, volume, share-of-wallet. Cheapest growth dollar.
- **Product Development**: new product to existing customer base. Leverages existing relationships, sales, brand.
- **Market Development**: existing product to new segment or geography. Watch for fit assumptions — what works in one segment doesn't always carry.
- **Diversification**: new product, new market. Highest risk and highest variance. Usually requires acquisition.

Rule of thumb: 70% of growth investment in penetration, 20% in adjacent (product or market dev), 10% in diversification. Inverting this ratio is how companies destroy capital.

## BCG Growth-Share Matrix

Classify business units (or product lines) on two dimensions:

- **Market growth rate** (high/low) — proxy for opportunity
- **Relative market share** (high/low) — proxy for competitive position

Four quadrants:

- **Stars** (high growth, high share): invest aggressively, capture share while market is forming. Cash-neutral or cash-negative.
- **Cash Cows** (low growth, high share): harvest. Cash-positive. Fund the rest of the portfolio.
- **Question Marks / Problem Children** (high growth, low share): decide — invest to gain share (become a Star) or divest (free up capital).
- **Dogs** (low growth, low share): divest or shut down. Rarely worth the management attention.

Limitations: assumes market share = profitability (often true but not always), ignores synergies between units, single dimension on each axis. Useful for portfolio allocation conversations, not as a literal scoring tool.

## Blue Ocean Strategy

Compete in uncontested market space by making competition irrelevant. Four actions on the strategy canvas:

- **Eliminate** — which factors that the industry takes for granted should be eliminated?
- **Reduce** — which factors should be reduced well below the industry standard?
- **Raise** — which factors should be raised well above the industry standard?
- **Create** — which factors should be created that the industry has never offered?

Examples: Cirque du Soleil eliminated animals and star performers, reduced fun/humor, raised theater quality and artistic value, created themed productions. They sold to a new audience (adults willing to pay theater prices) instead of competing with Ringling Brothers for kids.

Warning: blue oceans turn red. A successful blue ocean strategy invites imitation. Plan the next move while the current one is still working.

## Vertical and Horizontal Integration

**Vertical integration** — owning more of your value chain.
- *Upstream* (toward suppliers): control inputs, capture supplier margin, reduce dependency. Tesla's battery cells.
- *Downstream* (toward customers): control distribution, customer relationship, retail margin. Apple Stores.
- *When it works*: when the integrated activity is critical, the market for it is thin or unreliable, or there's a unique capability you can build.
- *When it doesn't*: when the integrated activity is a commodity, well-served by specialists, or distracts from your core capability.

**Horizontal integration** — acquiring competitors or adjacent companies at the same level of the value chain.
- *Why*: scale, geography expansion, talent, product gap-fill.
- *Watch for*: cultural integration cost, regulatory pushback, dis-synergies (the combined company is less than the sum of the parts).

## Strategy Diagnostic — A Forcing Question

"What do we explicitly choose NOT to do?" If you can't name three meaningful things, you don't have a strategy — you have a plan to do everything.

Good strategies are made of hard tradeoffs. If the leadership team can't name the tradeoffs out loud, the strategy isn't operating.
