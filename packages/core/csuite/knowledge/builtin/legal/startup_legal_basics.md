# Startup Legal Basics

*Note: This document provides general educational information about legal concepts commonly relevant to technology companies. It is not legal advice. Consult a licensed attorney for decisions affecting your company.*

## Corporate Structure

**Delaware C-Corp** is the standard for VC-backed startups. Reasons:
- Investor expectation — most institutional investors are structured to invest in Delaware C-corps
- Predictable law — Delaware corporate law is the most developed and understood
- Delaware Court of Chancery — specialized business court with experienced judges and no jury

Convert to C-corp before raising institutional capital. Conversions from LLCs are messier and more expensive than starting right.

## Intellectual Property Ownership

**The cardinal rule**: All IP created by employees and contractors must be assigned to the company.

**Employees**: Use a Proprietary Information and Inventions Agreement (PIIA) or Employment Confidentiality Agreement. Every employee should sign one on day one, before they start work. It covers:
- Assignment of all work product to the company
- Confidentiality of company information
- Non-solicitation (check enforceability in your state)

**Contractors**: A standard consulting agreement with an IP assignment clause. Without this, a contractor owns the work they create. This is a serious legal risk and investors will catch it in diligence.

**Prior IP**: If founders or employees bring pre-existing IP that the company will use, get a written license or assignment. Don't let important IP live outside the company.

## Equity and Cap Table

**Common stock**: Founders and employees
**Preferred stock**: Investors (with preferences over common in liquidation)

**Vesting**: Standard is 4-year vesting with a 1-year cliff. Cliff means nothing vests until year 1; then 25% vests at the cliff and the remainder vests monthly thereafter.

Founders should be on vesting schedules. Investors expect it. It also protects the company if a founder leaves early.

**Option pool**: Typically 10-20% of fully diluted shares reserved for employee equity. Usually created at a round of financing. Model the pool size based on your hiring plan for the next 18 months.

**Section 83(b) election**: Founders and early employees who receive unvested restricted stock should file an 83(b) election within 30 days of receiving the stock. Failure to file means paying ordinary income tax as shares vest (potentially on a much higher value). This is a critical tax planning step.

## Employment Classification

**Employee vs. Contractor**: Misclassification is a significant legal risk. Tests vary by state (California's ABC test is the strictest), but common factors:
- Does the company control how the work is performed? (Employee indicator)
- Is the work part of the company's core business? (Employee indicator)
- Does the person work for multiple clients? (Contractor indicator)

**Misclassification consequences**: Back taxes, penalties, benefits owed, class action exposure. Before classifying anyone as a contractor for ongoing work, get legal review.

## Key Contracts

**Term Sheets**: Not binding (except exclusivity and confidentiality clauses). Don't treat them as a done deal until definitive documents are signed.

**SAFEs (Simple Agreement for Future Equity)**: Common for pre-seed. Convert to equity at the next priced round. Key terms: valuation cap, discount rate. Post-money SAFEs (YC standard) are simpler to model.

**Customer Contracts**: SaaS MSA + Order Form is the standard structure. Key terms to own: limitation of liability (cap at 12 months of fees paid), indemnification scope, data ownership, auto-renewal, and termination rights.

**NDAs**: Use mutual NDAs for business discussions. One-way NDAs (you signing theirs) need review for scope — overly broad NDAs can prevent you from hiring their employees or building competing products.
