# Customer Interview Plan

The single highest-leverage discovery activity is customer interviews. The single most-common reason they're poorly done is that people skip the planning step and dive into asking questions. Plans produce learning; ad-hoc conversations produce confirmation bias.

## Inputs to gather first

Before drafting the plan, confirm or ask for:

1. **The research goal** — what specific question are we trying to answer? ("Is this segment a fit?" "What's their actual buying process?" "What would they not pay for?")
2. **The target audience** — current customers, prospects, churned, target ICP, etc.
3. **The product or feature in question** — if applicable
4. **The decision the research informs** — what will change based on what you learn
5. **Timeline and capacity** — how fast do you need it; how many interviews can you run

If any of these is missing, stop and ask. Research without a decision in mind produces information without action.

## The five interview types

Pick the right type for the research goal:

**1. Problem interviews** — explore a customer's pain
- Goal: understand the problem in depth before building solutions
- Pre-product, or when entering a new segment
- Open-ended; behavioral

**2. Switch interviews (JTBD)** — explore why a customer switched (or didn't)
- Goal: understand the forces that drive change in their workflow
- Used to discover what triggers a buying decision
- Structured around the "switch moment"

**3. Concept tests** — show a prototype or idea, gather reactions
- Goal: validate value perception and usability of a specific concept
- Mid-product development
- Mix of observation (watching them interact) and conversation

**4. Usability tests** — observe a customer using your product
- Goal: identify friction in a specific workflow
- Throughout product life
- Heavy on observation; light on conversation

**5. Win/loss interviews** — understand recent deal outcomes
- Goal: learn what drove a buying decision
- Post-deal (won or lost)
- Often run by a third party for objectivity

## Output structure

### 1. Research goal (1-2 sentences)

The specific question you're answering. The decision it informs.

Example: "Do mid-market RevOps leaders see value in an open-source AI executive vs. a SaaS one? Decision: whether to invest in self-hosted deployment as a primary positioning angle for the next 6 months."

### 2. Target audience and recruiting

- **Profile**: industry, size, role, situation (specific)
- **Number**: 6-12 typical (sub-5 not enough signal; over-15 diminishing returns per cycle)
- **Recruitment source**: customer base, prospect list, third-party panel (Respondent.io, UserInterviews), warm intros
- **Incentive**: typical $50-200 for an hour; more for senior roles; sometimes a charitable donation alternative

### 3. Interview script

Per type, the structure differs.

**For problem interviews (45-60 min):**

*Opening (5 min):*
- Thank them for time; explain context (research, not sales)
- Confirm OK to record
- Brief background on them (role, team, company)

*Their work (10-15 min):*
- "Walk me through your typical week"
- "What does [the relevant workflow] look like for you today?"
- "What tools and people are involved?"

*Specific recent example (15-20 min):*
- "Tell me about the last time you [did the relevant job]"
- "What was the trigger? What did you do first?"
- "What was hardest? What surprised you?"
- "How did it end? What was the outcome?"

*The aspirational (5-10 min):*
- "If a magic wand could fix one thing about that experience..."
- "What's NOT working in how you do this today?"

*Wrap (5 min):*
- "Anything you wish I'd asked about?"
- "Who else should I talk to?"
- Thank, share next steps

**Discipline:**
- Past behavior, not future intent ("what did you do" not "what would you do")
- Specific examples, not generalizations
- Listen more than talk; ratio 80/20 in their favor
- Probe answers ("tell me more about that", "what does that mean for you")
- Don't pitch the product

**For switch interviews:**
- Anchor on a specific switch event
- Walk backward from the switch (push, pull, anxiety, habit forces)
- Walk forward (what happened next, did they get the outcome)

**For concept tests:**
- Show the concept early
- Watch them interact (silent observation often more valuable than asking)
- "Talk out loud as you explore this"
- After: "What do you think this is for? What problem does it solve?"
- Don't lead with the value prop — see if they discover it

### 4. The Mom Test reminders

Rob Fitzpatrick's three rules:
1. **Talk about their life, not your idea**
2. **Ask about specifics in the past, not opinions about the future**
3. **Talk less, listen more**

Avoid:
- "Would you use a product that does X?" (yes-bias)
- "How much would you pay for X?" (pricing requires real intent, not hypothetical)
- "What features would you want?" (customers describe solutions; you need problems)

### 5. Synthesis approach

After all interviews:

- **Notes consolidated** in a shared doc — quotes preserved verbatim
- **Themes coded** — tag recurring concepts (problem types, behaviors, emotions)
- **Outliers noted** — surprising or anomalous responses
- **Insights extracted** — what's the pattern that emerges across interviews
- **Implications documented** — what should change based on this

Synthesis is the work. Most teams stop at "we did 10 interviews" and don't actually extract the insight.

### 6. Output artifact

The deliverable that turns research into decision input.

Typical format (3-5 pages):
- Research question and approach
- Who we talked to (anonymized)
- Top 3-5 themes (with supporting quotes)
- Surprising findings
- Implications and recommendations
- Next steps (further research, decisions to make)

Distribute to: product team, leadership, sales/CS (for context), the broader company if relevant.

## Discipline

- **Don't ask leading questions.** "Do you think this is useful?" → "Walk me through how you'd use this."
- **Specifics over generalizations.** "Tell me about the last time" beats "What do you usually do."
- **Note what they DON'T say.** Silence on topics you expected to come up is data.
- **PM in the interviews.** Outsourcing to a researcher loses the PM's contextual judgment.
- **Synthesize after every 3-4 interviews.** Don't wait until all interviews are done to start finding patterns.
- **Update the script as you learn.** Early interviews surface better questions for later ones.

## What NOT to do

- Pitch the product during the interview
- Argue with the customer's answers
- Give them the answer you want to hear
- Run 30 minutes when 60 was scheduled (cuts the rich middle)
- Skip recording (your memory will fail you)
- Interview only existing customers (selection bias — you only hear from people who chose you)
- Stop at "we did interviews" without synthesizing into change

## Common pitfalls

- **The fan customer trap**: interviewing your biggest fans tells you why fans love you. Doesn't tell you why others don't buy.
- **The vanity quote trap**: cherry-picking quotes that confirm your bias.
- **The "everyone said X" trap**: 2 people saying X is not "everyone."
- **The hypothetical trap**: customers are bad at predicting future behavior; ask about past behavior.

## Closing summary

End the plan with three sentences:

1. The research question and the decision it informs
2. The target audience and number of interviews
3. The timeline and the date the synthesis will be ready
