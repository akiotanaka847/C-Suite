# CEO Succession

The board's single most consequential decision is who runs the company. Done well, CEO succession looks seamless from outside. Done poorly, it's a years-long destabilization that costs market value, talent, and momentum.

## When Succession Conversations Begin

The right answer is: from day one of the current CEO's tenure.

**Why it matters:**
- The "hit by a bus" risk is real (illness, sudden departure, conduct issue)
- Internal succession requires years of development
- External searches done under time pressure are 3-5x more likely to fail
- Boards judged in hindsight on succession planning, not just operating results

**The trap most boards fall into:**
- Successful CEO; no perceived urgency
- Years pass with no documented succession plan
- CEO departs (planned or unplanned); board scrambles
- Interim CEO appointed; external search drags 9-18 months
- Often a disruptive external hire; performance suffers; CEO turnover repeats in 2-3 years

The companies that handle CEO succession well do it consciously, well before the need is acute.

## The Three Succession Categories

**1. Planned succession (the gold standard)**
- Current CEO and board agree on timeline (1-3 years out)
- Successor identified and developed
- Smooth handoff
- Examples: Cook from Jobs (Apple), Nadella's path to CEO (Microsoft)

**2. Performance-driven succession**
- Board loses confidence in CEO
- Performance documented over time
- Decision communicated; transition follows
- Higher disruption than planned

**3. Emergency succession**
- CEO unable to continue (death, illness, conduct issue, sudden departure)
- Interim CEO from internal ranks
- External search if no internal successor ready
- Maximum disruption

The board should be prepared for all three at all times.

## Internal Succession — Building the Bench

The best CEO successors are often inside the company. The discipline is developing them deliberately.

**Identifying potential CEO successors:**
- Strong operating results in current role
- Strategic thinking beyond their function
- Ability to lead and inspire across functions
- Cultural alignment with company values
- Demonstrated learning agility

**Common internal successor profiles:**
- *COO or President*: business leadership track; often the heir apparent in well-run cos
- *CRO / CSO*: revenue leadership; common in sales-led businesses
- *CTO / CPO*: product / technology leadership; common in product-led businesses
- *CFO*: financial leadership; less common as CEO but increasingly seen
- *Business unit GM*: P&L responsibility for a meaningful business; strong CEO indicator

**Development for CEO readiness:**
- Cross-functional rotations (run a function you haven't run)
- Board exposure (present to the board regularly; observe board dynamics)
- External board service (other company's board)
- Executive coaching
- Stretch assignments (M&A integration, geographic expansion, crisis leadership)
- Customer / investor relationship development

**Timeline:**
- Identify potential CEO successors 5-7 years out
- Develop 2-3 candidates in parallel (some will leave, some won't develop as hoped)
- Final selection 1-2 years before transition
- Public successor announcement when transition timing decided

**The "ready now" / "ready in 1-2 years" / "ready in 3-5 years" framework:**
- Annual board discussion: where is each candidate on this scale?
- Specific development plan to move each candidate forward
- Honest assessment — promotion isn't a participation trophy

## External Succession — When and How

**When to look externally:**
- No internal candidate ready and emergency is imminent
- Strategic shift requires different background than internal options
- Internal candidates have unanimously declined or are not viable
- Board / leadership desires fresh perspective

**The external search process:**
- Engage executive search firm (Heidrick, Spencer Stuart, Egon Zehnder, Korn Ferry)
- Search committee of board (often 3-5 directors)
- Candidate profile developed: experience, skills, culture, leadership style
- 3-6 month search typical
- Reference checks aggressively (CEO references must be deep — formal and back-channel)
- Final candidate vetted by full board

**Cost considerations:**
- Search firm fee: 25-33% of first-year cash comp
- Sign-on package: often $5-20M+ for established public-company CEO (cash + equity)
- Make-whole equity (replaces unvested equity left at prior employer)
- Severance package (often guaranteed 1-2 years comp if terminated without cause)

**Common external CEO mistakes:**
- Hiring based on prior company brand without diligence on actual results
- Inadequate cultural assessment (industry experience matches; cultural fit doesn't)
- Over-relying on the search firm's recommendations
- Insufficient onboarding investment (drops the new CEO into the deep end)

## Founder CEO Transitions

A specific case with unique dynamics.

**The patterns:**

*Founder steps to Chairman / CTO:*
- Founder retains strategic / product influence
- New CEO runs operations
- Works when: founder genuinely lets go of operations; new CEO has authority
- Fails when: founder second-guesses every decision; new CEO loses team's confidence

*Founder steps to Product / Innovation role:*
- Founder leads a domain they're passionate about (often product)
- New CEO has full operating responsibility
- Works when: clear role separation
- Fails when: founder's domain overlaps with CEO authority

*Founder departs entirely:*
- Rarely amicable initially
- Sometimes correct (founder not built for stage; strategic vision divergence)
- Equity, role, and communication terms heavily negotiated

**The honest founder-CEO conversation:**
- Often the lead independent director or chair leads
- "The company has evolved beyond what's most exciting for you; let's talk about what's next"
- Founders often know before they're told; the conversation just makes it explicit
- Avoid: surprise, public ambush, or perceived betrayal — usually fatal to relationship

**Equity and economic considerations:**
- Vesting acceleration (often partial)
- Continued advisory role with compensation
- Board seat retention (often, for some period)
- Public communication that protects founder's reputation
- Cap table considerations (founder remains significant shareholder)

## The Transition Period

The first 90-180 days of a new CEO determine the trajectory.

**Pre-transition:**
- Successor named publicly with timeline
- 30-90 day overlap with outgoing CEO (often)
- Successor meets all direct reports, key customers, key investors
- Listening tour: understand the business as it is

**Day 1:**
- Internal all-hands (introduction, vision, immediate priorities)
- External communication (press, customers, investors)
- One-on-ones scheduled with leadership team

**First 30 days:**
- Listening more than deciding
- Building relationships
- Understanding finances, people, customers
- Avoiding major decisions unless urgent

**Days 31-90:**
- First strategic clarifications (where we're going, what changes)
- Initial team adjustments (often 1-3 senior changes)
- First board meeting as CEO
- First quarterly business cycle

**Days 91-180:**
- Strategy clarification or shift
- Org design adjustments
- Key talent assessments
- Customer relationship deepening

**Common transition failures:**
- Outgoing CEO undermines the new CEO (publicly or privately)
- New CEO makes too many changes too fast (loses credibility before building it)
- Board over-engages or under-engages
- Communication failures internally (rumor mill fills vacuum)

## The Outgoing CEO

Often overlooked: the human and operational management of the outgoing CEO.

**Departure-related elements:**
- Public narrative (what story does the company tell?)
- Equity treatment (acceleration, exercise window)
- Severance / advisory consulting arrangement
- Continued board role (often, for transition period)
- External commitments (other boards, advisory roles)
- Communications support (PR coordination, customer comms)

**Risks:**
- Disgruntled former CEO talking publicly
- Lawsuits over equity or comp treatment
- Talent following the former CEO (especially if they start a competing company)
- Cultural memory ("when [former CEO] was here...")

**Mitigation:**
- Generous treatment in exchange for cooperation
- Mutual non-disparagement
- Aligned public narrative
- Real role for the founder/CEO post-departure (if appropriate)

## Communications

CEO transitions are watched closely by employees, customers, investors, and competitors.

**Audiences and messages:**

*Employees:*
- Why the change (honest within reason)
- What's not changing (mission, strategy, key bets)
- What to expect in the transition
- How they should think about it

*Customers (especially top accounts):*
- Direct outreach from CEO + account team
- Reassurance of continuity
- Introduction to new CEO when appropriate
- Address concerns proactively

*Investors:*
- Board prep before announcement
- Direct calls to major shareholders
- Public statement with strategic continuity narrative
- Earnings call (if public) careful management of forward narrative

*Press:*
- Joint statement (often) from outgoing and new CEO
- Coordinated message
- Anticipate questions about why and what's next

**The "amicable departure" framing:**
- Sometimes true; sometimes face-saving
- Either way, message discipline matters
- Inconsistent stories surface eventually and damage trust

## The Board's Role During Transition

- Stability during the transition
- Visible support for the new CEO
- Active engagement with customers/investors as needed
- Resisting the urge to micromanage
- Letting the new CEO establish their leadership

**Common board mistakes:**
- Acting as shadow CEO during transition
- Public dissent on the choice
- Excessive interference with new CEO's decisions
- Failing to course-correct quickly when transition isn't working

## The Succession Diagnostic

For your board, annually:
1. If the CEO became unavailable tomorrow, who's the interim? Are they ready?
2. Who are our 2-3 internal CEO successors? What's their development plan?
3. When did we last have a substantive succession conversation in executive session?
4. Has the CEO participated in the succession planning, or is it being done around them?
5. What's our planned CEO timeline — and does the CEO know it?

If you can't answer #1 confidently, you have an emergency-succession exposure. The cost of building the answer is small; the cost of needing it without one is enormous.
