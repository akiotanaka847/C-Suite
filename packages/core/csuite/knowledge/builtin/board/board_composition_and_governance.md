# Board Composition and Governance

The board you assemble shapes the decisions you make, the capital you can raise, and the company you become. A well-composed board adds capability; a poorly composed board adds friction.

## Board Composition by Stage

**Pre-Seed / Seed (3 members):**
- 2 founders
- 1 lead investor (Series Seed or large angel)

The board is light. Decisions move fast. Founders retain control. This is the easiest governance stage.

**Series A (3-5 members):**
- 2 founders
- 1 Series A lead investor
- 1-2 independent directors

This is the most consequential composition. The first independent director sets the tone for the next several years. Choose deliberately.

**Series B (5-7 members):**
- 1-2 founders (often loses one seat)
- 2 institutional investors (Seed/A and Series B)
- 2-3 independent directors

Power dynamics shift. Founders become a minority of the board. This is when "board management" becomes a distinct skill.

**Late-stage / Pre-IPO (7-9 members):**
- 1 founder (often CEO seat)
- 2-3 investors
- 3-5 independent directors (often including a Lead Independent Director)

Public-company readiness requires more independents, audit committee, comp committee, governance committee.

**Public (typically 9-12 members):**
- Predominantly independent
- Specific committee structures required by exchange listing rules

## What Makes a Great Independent Director

The best independent directors bring one of three things:

**1. Specific operational expertise the company is missing**
- Former CMO if marketing is a gap
- Former CFO if you're approaching IPO
- Former GM of an industry vertical you're entering
- Their value is operational pattern-matching plus their network

**2. A network you can't access**
- Customer relationships at scale (former enterprise buyer)
- Investor relationships (former GP or repeat board member)
- Talent relationships (former CHRO from a target source company)
- Best deployed for: hiring, fundraising, customer intros

**3. Governance experience**
- Has navigated public-company readiness
- Has been on boards through M&A, CEO transitions, executive scandals
- Knows what good audit and comp processes look like
- Best deployed for: chair roles, lead independent, audit committee chair

**Bad independent directors:**
- Trophy hires (famous name, no time)
- Sole-issue advocates (one ideology drives every comment)
- Investors-without-the-money (they take a board seat instead of investing — usually because they couldn't write a check)
- "Friends of the founder" — useful early, often the wrong people once stakes are high

**Compensation:**
- Cash retainer: $10-50k/year (varies with stage and time commitment)
- Equity: 0.25-1.0% of fully-diluted at seed/A; smaller % at later stages
- Vested over 4 years; cliff structure
- Increases for committee chairs and lead independent

**Time commitment:**
- 4 board meetings/year, ~3 hours each
- 1-2 hours prep per meeting (read materials)
- Ad-hoc CEO check-ins (~1 hour/month)
- Total: 30-50 hours/year

## Fiduciary Duties

Board members owe fiduciary duties to the corporation. The two primary duties:

**Duty of Care** — exercise the care that a reasonably prudent person would in similar circumstances.
- Read the materials before meetings
- Ask informed questions
- Rely on management and experts (but verify when stakes are high)
- Take time to deliberate on major decisions

**Duty of Loyalty** — act in the interests of the corporation, not personal interests.
- Disclose conflicts of interest
- Recuse from votes where conflicted
- Don't divert corporate opportunities to yourself
- Maintain confidentiality

**The Business Judgment Rule** protects board decisions made in good faith, with adequate information, free of conflicts. Courts won't second-guess business outcomes; they will scrutinize process.

**Where fiduciary duties get tested:**
- M&A transactions (duty to maximize shareholder value)
- CEO compensation and equity grants
- Approving related-party transactions
- Handling allegations of misconduct
- Tradeoffs between common and preferred shareholders

For VC-backed boards: the duty is to the *corporation*, not to the investor that placed the director. This creates tension when investor and common shareholder interests diverge — most acutely in down-round financings and acquisitions where the preference stack exceeds the deal value.

## Board Committees

For larger boards (5+ members), committees handle work that's inefficient at the full board level.

**Audit Committee** (required pre-IPO):
- 2-3 independent directors, at least one financial expert
- Oversees financial reporting, external auditor, internal controls
- Meets 4x per year, often the day before board meetings

**Compensation Committee:**
- 2-3 independent directors
- Sets executive compensation, approves equity plans
- For public companies, oversees say-on-pay and Compensation Discussion & Analysis disclosure

**Governance / Nominating Committee:**
- 2-3 independent directors
- Identifies and recommends new directors
- Oversees board evaluation processes

**Special Committees** (ad-hoc):
- For specific situations (M&A negotiation, internal investigation, related-party transaction)
- Membership designed for independence and expertise relevant to the specific matter

Most growth-stage companies operate with just an Audit Committee, sometimes a Comp Committee. Other committees emerge as the company matures.

## Board Meeting Cadence and Structure

**Frequency:**
- Seed/early A: every 6-8 weeks
- Series A/B: quarterly (4x/year)
- Later stage: quarterly plus monthly investor updates

**Duration:**
- 2-4 hours per meeting
- Annual strategic planning session (full day, often offsite) — separate from operational reviews

**Standard meeting structure** (see also `board_communication.md`):
1. Executive summary (CEO POV, 5 min)
2. Business update with metrics vs. plan (20-30 min)
3. Two deep-dive strategic topics (60-80 min total)
4. Decisions and approvals (10-15 min)
5. Executive session (CEO and other execs leave, board discusses management — 15 min)

**Pre-read:**
- Sent 5-7 days in advance
- Board members are expected to have read materials
- The meeting is for discussion, not presentation

## Board Evaluation

Mature boards self-evaluate annually:
- Effectiveness of board composition (do we have the right people?)
- Quality of meetings (are we discussing the right things at the right depth?)
- Director effectiveness (anonymous feedback per director)
- CEO assessment of the board's contribution

This is uncomfortable but valuable. The boards that resist self-evaluation are usually the ones that need it most.

## CEO-Board Relationship Management

**The relationship is the CEO's responsibility to manage.**

- Schedule 1:1s with each board member quarterly (more for the active ones)
- Brief on material issues between meetings — never let a board member be surprised by something they read in a press release
- Use them for the things they're best at (intros, advice, recruiting, governance), not for things that aren't their core value (operational tactics)

**Board members are not your friends, but they should be your allies.** The CEO who treats the board adversarially loses; the CEO who treats them as part of the team gets the value.

**When CEO-board trust breaks:**
- Repeated missed numbers without credible explanations
- Surprise bad news ("we found out from a reporter")
- Perceived shading of the truth
- Major strategic shifts presented as fait accompli without consultation

Repair is possible but takes 6-12 months of consistent reset. Many CEOs don't get the time.

## Removing or Replacing Board Members

The most uncomfortable governance topic. Sometimes necessary.

**Common reasons:**
- Director is consistently unprepared, uninformed, or absent
- Director's expertise no longer matches the company's stage
- Director is destructive to board dynamics
- Conflict of interest has emerged

**Removal mechanics:**
- Investor seats: typically tied to share class; require investor's consent to vacate or replace
- Independent seats: removal requires shareholder vote (per charter); negotiated departure is more common
- Term limits: increasingly common in modern bylaws (3-year terms, max 2-3 terms)

**The conversation:**
- Should come from the board chair or lead independent, not the CEO
- Frame as "the company has evolved and we need different expertise"
- Allow graceful exit ("not seeking re-election")
- Maintain the relationship — former directors can still help

## The Critical Test

Once a year, the leadership team and the chair should ask:

*"If we were composing this board from scratch today, who would we choose? Who's already on it, and who isn't?"*

The gap between the answer and the current board is your governance roadmap.
