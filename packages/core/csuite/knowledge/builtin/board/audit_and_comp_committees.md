# Audit and Compensation Committees

Most board work happens in committee, not in full board meetings. The audit and compensation committees are the two most consequential. Done well, they provide focused oversight that the full board can't. Done badly, they become rubber-stamp ceremonies that miss material issues.

## Why Committees Exist

The full board can't deeply review every financial statement, audit finding, and compensation decision. Committees:

- Develop expertise in their domain
- Conduct deep work between full board meetings
- Make decisions delegated to them (and recommendations for full board approval)
- Provide independent oversight where conflicts could exist

For public companies, audit committee is required by exchange listing rules. Compensation committee is required for many decisions. For private companies, both become important pre-IPO and are best practice earlier.

## Audit Committee

The committee with the highest fiduciary stakes. Misses here are typically the most expensive.

**Composition:**
- 3+ directors (public requires); 2-3 typical for private growth-stage
- All independent (public requires; best practice for private)
- At least one "financial expert" (specific definition in SEC rules)
- Should include directors with: financial reporting experience, audit committee experience, industry-specific knowledge

**Charter (the foundational document):**
- Scope and authority
- Required reviews
- Meeting cadence
- External auditor relationship
- Internal audit function (if any)
- Whistleblower complaint handling
- Cyber and risk oversight (increasingly included)

**Standard meeting agenda items:**

*Pre-quarter / pre-year:*
- External auditor planning (scope, materiality, areas of focus)
- Internal control testing approach
- Risk assessment updates

*Post-quarter / post-year:*
- Financial statement review with CFO and external auditor
- Audit findings and management responses
- Going concern assessment
- Critical accounting policies and estimates
- Significant unusual transactions
- Internal control issues identified

*Ongoing:*
- Compliance and regulatory matters
- Whistleblower complaints (status, investigations, conclusions)
- Cyber risk and incidents
- Major vendor / financial system changes
- Tax matters

**The external auditor relationship:**
- Audit committee, not management, owns this relationship
- Annual auditor selection / re-engagement
- Negotiation of audit fees
- Review of auditor independence
- Periodic rotation of audit partners (required for public; best practice for private)
- Pre-approval of non-audit services from the auditor (avoid independence issues)

**Executive sessions with auditors:**
- Standard practice: audit committee meets with external auditors WITHOUT management present
- "Anything you want to tell us that you couldn't say in front of management?"
- Surfaces issues that management filters out

**Common audit committee failures:**

1. *Going through the motions* — reviewing financial statements as a ceremony, not a substantive review
2. *Insufficient expertise* — no member who can credibly challenge the CFO's accounting judgments
3. *No executive session with auditors* — missing the most valuable hour of the meeting
4. *Whistleblower complaints not surfaced* — process exists on paper, doesn't function
5. *Cyber risk treated as IT problem* — material risk not getting board-level attention
6. *Audit fees negotiated by CFO* — independence compromise (audit committee should negotiate)

## Compensation Committee

The committee setting the tone for how the company pays. Highly visible (especially post-IPO via CD&A disclosure).

**Composition:**
- 3+ directors (public requires); 2-3 typical for private growth-stage
- All independent
- Often includes: former HR / People Officer, current or former CEO from peer company, finance expertise

**Charter:**
- Executive compensation philosophy
- CEO compensation determination
- Other executive compensation review (typically CEO recommends; comp committee reviews)
- Equity plan administration
- Share repurchase programs (sometimes)
- Diversity and pay equity oversight

**Standard meeting agenda items:**

*Annual:*
- Comp philosophy review
- Peer group review (which companies do we benchmark to?)
- CEO performance assessment and comp decision
- Executive team performance review and comp adjustments
- Annual equity grant approvals
- Annual bonus payout determinations
- Pay equity analysis

*As needed:*
- New executive hire compensation
- Departing executive severance and equity treatment
- Significant equity plan changes
- Stock split, dividend, or recap impact on outstanding equity

**Compensation philosophy decisions:**
- Target percentile vs. peer group (50th, 75th, 90th)
- Mix of cash vs. equity vs. variable
- Performance metrics for variable comp (revenue, EBITDA, FCF, NPS, ESG)
- Equity vehicle mix (options, RSUs, PSUs)
- Vesting schedules (4-year, performance-tied)
- Clawback provisions (mandatory for public companies post-Dodd-Frank)

**Peer group selection:**
- Companies of similar size, growth, and industry
- 8-20 companies typical
- Updated annually
- Public companies (compensation is disclosed; data available)

**Pay equity oversight:**
- Gender pay equity analysis
- Race / ethnicity pay equity analysis (where data permits)
- Equal pay reviews aligned with regulatory requirements
- Disclosure (UK gender pay gap, etc.)

**Common compensation committee failures:**

1. *Peer group gaming* — selecting comparables that justify higher pay
2. *Rubber-stamping CEO recommendations* — for other executives without independent review
3. *Pay-for-performance disconnect* — comp paid even when performance lags
4. *Insider transactions* — executive loans (illegal for public companies), related-party deals
5. *Equity grants without performance conditions* — pure time-vesting at senior levels
6. *No pay equity analysis* — discovering disparity in public disclosure, not in committee meeting

## Other Common Board Committees

**Governance / Nominating Committee:**
- New director identification and recommendation
- Board evaluation processes
- Governance policies and updates
- Director compensation
- Required for public; less common for private

**Strategy / Finance Committee:**
- Capital structure
- M&A oversight (initial evaluation)
- Major capital deployment
- Less common; sometimes appropriate for complex portfolios

**Risk Committee:**
- Enterprise risk management
- Often combined with audit at smaller companies
- Increasingly separate at financial services and large enterprises

**Technology / Cybersecurity Committee:**
- Emerging committee at large companies
- Major technology decisions and risks
- Cyber posture oversight

**Special Committees:**
- Ad-hoc for specific situations (M&A negotiation, internal investigation, related-party transaction)
- Membership designed for independence and relevant expertise
- Disbanded after specific matter resolves

## Committee Operations

**Meeting cadence:**
- Audit committee: typically 4x per year (quarterly), often the day before board meeting
- Comp committee: typically 3-4x per year, plus ad-hoc
- Other committees: as needed (quarterly or less)

**Meeting structure:**
- Pre-read circulated 5-7 days in advance
- Agenda with time allocations
- Executive session at the end (committee members only)
- Minutes captured by corporate secretary
- Committee reports out to full board

**Documentation:**
- Charters reviewed and updated annually
- Minutes are official board records
- Decisions properly documented for regulatory and legal purposes

**Chair selection:**
- Committee chair typically a board member with deep expertise
- Audit chair: often a CFO or former auditor
- Comp chair: often a former CHRO, CEO, or experienced compensation expert

## Private vs. Public Committee Practices

**Private company:**
- Often informal committee structure (some meetings, less rigorous documentation)
- Audit committee value: cleaner audits, fewer surprises, IPO readiness
- Comp committee value: independent voice on executive comp, avoiding founder over-pay

**Pre-IPO:**
- Formalize committees 12-18 months before IPO
- Establish charters, regular meetings, minutes discipline
- Implement requirements that will be mandatory post-IPO (CFO certification, internal audit, etc.)

**Public:**
- Required committees with required charters
- SEC disclosure of committee composition, meetings, member expertise
- Required disclosures around executive comp (CD&A)
- Required certifications (SOX 302 and 404)

## Working With Committees as CEO / CFO

Both committees significantly affect the C-suite. Working effectively with them:

**For CEOs:**
- Treat audit and comp as serious oversight, not adversaries
- Surface issues early (better to lose face on the issue than be discovered hiding it)
- Pre-discuss material items with committee chair before meetings
- Don't try to control or filter what reaches the committee — counterproductive long-term

**For CFOs:**
- Audit committee is a key relationship; develop trust early
- Be candid in executive sessions (auditor present without other management)
- Communicate proactively about emerging accounting issues
- Quality of materials matters — a sloppy audit committee package erodes trust faster than substantive issues

## The Committee Diagnostic

For each committee in your structure:
1. Does the charter accurately reflect current scope and responsibilities?
2. Do members have the expertise to credibly challenge management?
3. Are executive sessions actually held — with substantive discussion?
4. Are decisions properly documented — would they withstand legal/regulatory scrutiny?
5. What did the committee NOT discuss this year that it should have?

#5 is the most-illuminating question. The work the committee avoided is where the next problem will surface.
