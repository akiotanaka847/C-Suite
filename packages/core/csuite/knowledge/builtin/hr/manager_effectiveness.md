# Manager Effectiveness

A great manager multiplies the impact of their team. A mediocre manager is a tax on it. The single largest source of variance in employee experience, performance, and retention is the immediate manager — not the company.

## What Managers Actually Do (and Most Don't)

The work of a manager, in priority order:

1. **Set clear expectations** — what good looks like, what's NOT in scope, what the priorities are
2. **Remove obstacles** — clear the path so the team can execute
3. **Give honest, useful feedback** — both reinforcing and corrective, in real-time
4. **Develop the team** — hire well, coach for growth, manage out misfits
5. **Make decisions** — own the calls that need to be made, don't punt them upward
6. **Communicate strategy and context** — explain why, not just what

The work most managers actually do (and shouldn't):
- Status meetings and reporting
- Approving things that could be self-service
- Re-doing work the team did imperfectly
- Avoiding hard conversations

The shift from individual contributor to manager requires letting the IC work be done by the team — and resisting the urge to do it yourself when you could do it faster. That urge is what limits a manager's scaling capacity.

## The 1:1 — Highest Leverage 30 Minutes

The most underused tool in management. Done well, 1:1s prevent 80% of problems before they escalate.

**Format that works:**
- 30 minutes, weekly (biweekly minimum)
- The report owns the agenda, not the manager
- Shared running doc: agenda items, action items, key decisions
- Phone or walk if possible — different dynamic than video

**Standard agenda:**
1. *How are you?* (Real, not perfunctory. 2-3 minutes.)
2. *What's on your mind?* (Their topics first. 15-20 minutes.)
3. *What's on my mind?* (Manager topics, including feedback. 5-10 minutes.)
4. *Action items + next 1:1 timing.* (2-3 minutes.)

**Topics to cover regularly:**
- What's working / not working in their role
- Blockers (and how the manager can unblock)
- Career growth / development
- Feedback (in both directions)
- Strategic context (what's happening at the company / team level)

**Topics NOT to cover:**
- Project status updates (separate meetings or async)
- Group decisions (don't make group decisions in 1:1s; it surprises the rest of the team)
- Performance-issue escalation (those need explicit, documented conversations — not slipped into a regular 1:1)

**The single best question:** "What's the most important thing we should be talking about right now?" Forces priority surfacing.

## Feedback — The Manager's Core Currency

Feedback that lands and changes behavior is the single biggest indicator of manager skill.

**Four conditions for feedback that works:**
1. **Specific** — observable behavior or output, not character judgment
2. **Timely** — within days, not at quarterly review
3. **Actionable** — the recipient knows what to do differently
4. **In the right setting** — corrective feedback private; reinforcing feedback often public

**The SBI model (Situation-Behavior-Impact):**
- *Situation*: "In yesterday's customer call..."
- *Behavior*: "...when the customer raised the pricing concern, you cut them off and started pitching."
- *Impact*: "The customer disengaged for the rest of the call and we lost the close."

Then ask: "What's your read?" — engage their perspective. End with explicit agreement on what changes.

**Reinforcing feedback (the underused half):**
- Most managers give 4-5x more corrective feedback than reinforcing
- Reinforcing feedback shapes behavior at least as much as corrective
- Be specific: "The way you opened that meeting with the customer's pain — that's exactly how to do it. Worth doing every time."

**Asking for feedback (the most underused entirely):**
- "What's one thing I could do to make me a better manager for you?"
- Most reports won't answer the first time. Keep asking.
- Then: act on what you hear. Otherwise stop asking.

## Performance Management

The big-picture rhythm (specific tactics in `performance_management.md`):

**Continuous:**
- Real-time feedback in 1:1s
- Address performance issues in the week they occur, not the quarter

**Quarterly:**
- Goal/OKR review
- Brief written check-in (1 page) — what did we accomplish, what's next, any concerns

**Annually:**
- Formal performance review
- Compensation discussion (separate conversation)
- 360-degree feedback (every 1-2 years)

**The standard most managers fall short of:**
- No surprise in the annual review — every performance issue should have been raised, documented, and worked on already
- If the annual review contains new information for the report, the manager failed

## Coaching vs. Telling

The shift from senior IC to manager often requires the shift from telling to coaching.

**Telling:**
- Faster in the short term
- Creates dependency — the team can't operate without you
- Limits the team's growth

**Coaching:**
- Slower in the short term
- Builds capability — the team can handle increasingly hard situations
- Scales — the manager can handle larger teams

**Coaching questions (instead of telling):**
- "What have you tried?"
- "What would you do if I weren't available?"
- "What's the risk if we go your way?"
- "What's the worst that happens?"
- "What would you need to know to decide?"

**When to tell rather than coach:**
- Urgent decisions where time costs more than learning
- Clear right answers the team genuinely cannot reach
- New employees in their first 90 days — they need scaffolding

**The 70/30 split:** ~70% coaching, ~30% telling. Most managers are inverted.

## Delegation

Delegation is not assigning tasks — it's transferring ownership of outcomes.

**Levels of delegation (loosely from Marquet's Turn the Ship Around):**

1. *Wait until told what to do*
2. *Ask what to do*
3. *Recommend, then do*
4. *Do, and immediately report*
5. *Do, and report periodically*
6. *Do (you have full authority)*

Most teams operate at levels 1-3. Healthy teams should mostly operate at 4-6, with 1-3 reserved for genuinely new or high-stakes situations.

**Delegation discipline:**
- State the level explicitly ("you own this; I want to know if X happens but otherwise you decide")
- Match the level to the person's competence in this specific area (delegation level varies by topic per person)
- When something goes wrong, don't immediately ratchet down delegation — diagnose what happened first
- Increase delegation deliberately as competence grows

## Hiring on a Manager's Team

The single most important skill a manager has is hiring well. A hiring mistake costs 18 months of management time and team morale, plus the lost opportunity of who else you could have hired.

**Manager-specific hiring discipline:**
- Be ruthless on bar — one B-player drags down two A-players
- Reference check aggressively (see `hiring_frameworks.md`)
- Hire for the role the team needs next, not the role you needed last quarter
- Diversity of background matters; homogeneous teams underperform on novel problems

**When to manage out:**
- It's been 2-3 months since you flagged the performance issue and no improvement
- The behavior is destructive to others on the team
- You can't credibly imagine them succeeding in this role
- Diagnosis: have you been clear about expectations? Have they had real coaching? Do they want to succeed?

Most "termination decisions" are actually decisions to have been made 3-6 months earlier. Most regret is about delay, not action.

## Managing Up

Managing up is your manager's career as a person reporting to leadership.

**Discipline:**
- Bring solutions, not just problems
- Communicate proactively — if your manager hears something from your peer or report before you, that's a failure
- Make their job easier; this is the fastest path to autonomy and growth
- Disagree respectfully; commit fully once decided

**What NOT to do:**
- Surprise your manager with bad news in a meeting in front of others
- Loop in their manager without telling them first
- Complain about your manager publicly (in 1:1s with peers, in Slack)

## The Manager's Time Allocation

Where managers should spend their time, roughly:

- 30% — 1:1s, hiring conversations, performance discussions (people)
- 25% — strategic work (planning, prioritization, cross-functional)
- 20% — meetings (cross-functional, leadership team, customer-facing)
- 15% — communication (writing, presentations, async updates)
- 10% — IC contribution (where it adds unique value, not where you're doing the team's work)

Managers who are 50%+ on IC work are not managing — they're doing their old job with a new title. The team is unmanaged.

## Common Manager Failures

1. **Hands-off manager** — doesn't know what the team is doing, can't give meaningful feedback. "I trust my team" is sometimes code for "I don't know what's happening."
2. **Micromanager** — over-involved in every decision; team can't grow; manager is a bottleneck.
3. **Conflict-avoider** — won't have hard conversations; underperformance festers; high performers leave.
4. **Player-coach trap** — doing the IC work AND failing at the manager work.
5. **The promoted-too-soon manager** — great IC, not yet ready to manage; learns at the team's expense.

## The Manager Diagnostic

For each of your direct reports, weekly, ask yourself:
1. Do they know what's expected of them this week?
2. Have I unblocked anything for them?
3. Have I given them feedback (reinforcing OR corrective) in the last 7 days?
4. Do I know what they're worried about?
5. Are they growing — and can I name one specific way I'm developing them?

If you answer no to most of these consistently, you're not managing — you're administrating. The team will eventually leave (the good ones first).
