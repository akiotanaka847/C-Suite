# Layoffs and Reductions in Force

*Note: This document provides general operational guidance. Employment law varies materially by jurisdiction. Consult employment counsel before executing any layoff.*

A layoff is one of the hardest things a leader does. Done well, it preserves the company's future and treats departing employees with dignity. Done badly, it damages morale, brand, and legal exposure for years. The difference is preparation and execution discipline — not the layoff itself.

## When a Layoff Is the Right Decision

Layoffs are appropriate when:

1. **The business model has changed structurally** — costs need to align with new revenue trajectory; not a temporary downturn
2. **The cost reduction can't be achieved through attrition** — natural departures won't get there in the time available
3. **You've already cut non-people costs first** (contractors, vendors, marketing, travel, real estate)
4. **The cut size is meaningful** — usually 10%+ of headcount; smaller cuts often do more damage than they save

Layoffs are NOT appropriate when:
- The cost target could be hit with hiring freeze + non-payroll cuts
- The business performance issue is short-term and likely to reverse in 1-2 quarters
- The motivation is to fire specific people you should have managed out individually
- The motivation is signaling discipline to investors more than actual business need

## The Pre-Decision Work

Before announcing or even broadly discussing a layoff:

**Financial work:**
- Model the cost reduction needed
- Model the severance cost (one-time hit) vs. ongoing savings
- Calculate runway impact in base, downside, upside scenarios
- Model the business impact (which functions, which projects affected)

**Strategic work:**
- Define the future-state org — what does the company look like after?
- Decide which functions / teams / roles are affected
- Decide reduction percentage by function (not always uniform)
- Identify must-keep talent regardless of function

**Legal work:**
- Engage employment counsel early
- Identify WARN Act applicability (US federal — typically 100+ employees, 50+ affected)
- Identify state-specific notice requirements (mini-WARN acts)
- Identify protected class considerations (adverse impact analysis)
- Plan severance and release agreements

**Communication work:**
- Draft all-hands message
- Draft customer / partner communications
- Draft press / external communications
- Prepare manager guidance and FAQs

## The Selection Process

**Selection criteria (in this order):**
1. *Role elimination* — entire functions or teams being eliminated; affected employees are everyone in that scope
2. *Strategic fit* — does this role advance the new strategy?
3. *Performance and capability* — within affected functions, retain the strongest performers
4. *Specific skill needs* — keep critical specialist knowledge

**Process discipline:**
- Document the selection rationale per person (defensible if legally challenged)
- Adverse impact analysis: does the selection disproportionately affect protected classes?
- If yes, justify with role-elimination logic OR adjust selections to mitigate
- Consult employment counsel on the analysis before finalizing the list

**The hardest selection:** when two people are roughly equivalent and one must be cut. Don't avoid the decision; the team will see the avoidance and lose trust. Use defensible criteria (recent performance ratings, specific skill match, role versatility) and document.

## Severance Structure

Severance economics matter — both financially for the departing employee and reputationally for the company.

**Typical severance components:**

- *Severance pay*: typically 2-4 weeks per year of service; sometimes minimum (8-12 weeks); senior employees get more (often 3-6 months)
- *Healthcare continuation*: COBRA paid by company for severance period (US); equivalent for other geographies
- *Equity treatment*: standard is forfeiture of unvested; some companies accelerate vested-but-unexercised window; some accelerate vesting for tenure milestones
- *Outplacement services*: 3-6 month subscription to career services (Lee Hecht Harrison, RiseSmart)
- *Reference policy*: clear policy on what references the company will provide
- *Mutual non-disparagement*: standard in release agreements

**Release agreement:**
- In exchange for severance, employee signs release of claims against the company
- Required revocation period (7 days under US federal age law) for employees 40+
- Has to be written in plain English (per ADEA for older employees)
- Employee should have time and opportunity to consult counsel

**Equity treatment is often the most-debated:**
- Standard: 90-day post-termination exercise window for vested options
- Some companies extend to 5-10 years post-termination (more employee-friendly)
- Acquihired or laid-off employees often get vesting acceleration (1-3 months)

## The Layoff Day Itself

The day requires military-level precision. Mistakes compound emotionally and legally.

**Day-before:**
- Final list locked
- All severance packages prepared and ready to print
- Manager training session: who does what, what to say, what NOT to say
- Communication channels prepared (Slack, email, all-hands)
- HR team on standby for individual conversations
- IT prepared to revoke access (timing varies by approach)

**The morning:**
- Affected employees notified first, individually, by their manager + HR (or HR + senior manager)
- Notification conversation: short, clear, kind ("We're making changes; today is your last day; here's your package; here's our HR person to help with details")
- Severance package handed over physically or via email
- IT revokes access during the notification window
- 30-60 minutes per notification typically

**Mid-morning all-hands:**
- CEO addresses the entire company
- Explain the why (business context)
- Explain the what (scope, who's affected, when)
- Explain the how (severance philosophy, support being provided)
- Acknowledge the emotional weight
- Be present for questions (or commit to a Q&A within 24-48 hours)

**Afternoon:**
- Affected employees pack and depart
- Remaining team has space (no expectation of productivity)
- Customer / partner outreach for relationships affected
- External communication (if material)

## Communication — The Most-Often-Mishandled Part

**Tone discipline:**
- Direct and honest about the why
- Empathetic without being patronizing
- Specific about what's changing
- Doesn't over-promise about the future ("this is the last one!" rings hollow)

**The all-hands message structure:**
1. The decision and scope (what)
2. The business context (why)
3. How affected employees are being treated (severance, support)
4. What's not changing (mission, strategy, key bets that remain)
5. What's expected of remaining team in the next days/weeks
6. Acknowledgment and gratitude to departing employees

**What NOT to say:**
- "We're stronger as a company today" (in the moment of the layoff, this is gaslighting)
- "These are difficult decisions but the right ones" (without explaining why)
- Vague promises about the future
- Diminishment of departing employees ("we'll all be more focused")

**Customer / partner communication:**
- Pre-draft for accounts where relationships are affected
- Customer success owns the outreach; CEO involvement for top accounts
- Acknowledge change without dramatizing it
- Reinforce continuity of service and account ownership

**Press / external:**
- For material layoffs (>10% of headcount, or affecting public profile): proactive press communication
- Tone: honest about business reality, respectful of affected employees, forward-looking without over-promising
- Brief executives and recruiters externally; departing employees will tell their story regardless

## The Remaining Team — Survivor Syndrome

The team that remains is not "lucky." They're traumatized.

**Predictable reactions:**
- Survivor guilt
- Loss of trust (in leadership, in the company)
- Increased workload (some work doesn't disappear with the people)
- Fear of "next round"
- Reduced risk-taking
- Active or passive job searching

**Leadership response:**
- Acknowledge the loss publicly and personally
- Be visible — leaders need to show up, not retreat to closed-door meetings
- Specifically address: is there more? (If you can't promise no more, don't — but address the question)
- Re-validate the path forward — what are we now, what's the plan
- Watch for departures of A-players in the next 6-9 months

**Manager 1:1 priorities in the weeks after:**
- Each person individually: how are you? what are you worried about? what do you need?
- Take notes; follow through; loop back

**Tactical relief:**
- Re-scope work explicitly — don't pretend the same amount of work can be done by fewer people
- Sunset projects rather than accelerate them
- Be transparent about which work is now lower priority

## Common Layoff Failures

1. **Too small a cut** — having to do a second round 6 months later is materially worse than one larger cut
2. **Selecting based on relationships, not criteria** — exposes you legally and demoralizes the team
3. **Leaking before announcement** — affected employees hear from peers, not from their manager
4. **Manager scripts not rehearsed** — emotional managers say things they shouldn't
5. **Severance announced AFTER the cut** — employees feel like terms could change; trust shattered
6. **Hiring back to the same roles weeks later** — the layoff was clearly wrong; trust never recovers
7. **No customer communication** — accounts find out from departed employees; relationship damaged
8. **Executive layoffs handled differently than rank-and-file** — public-facing severance disparities seed cynicism

## After-Action Review

Within 30 days, leadership team should review:
- Did we cut deep enough?
- What did we get wrong in execution (selection, communication, timing, severance)?
- What's the post-cut team morale data telling us?
- Who's at risk of leaving (regrettable attrition tracking)?
- What process do we need before the next major workforce action?

The lessons get embedded in the playbook for next time. Most companies have multiple layoff events over their lifetime; the second is rarely much better than the first because lessons aren't institutionalized.

## The Diagnostic Before Any Layoff

Before pulling the trigger:
1. Have we cut non-people costs first? (List them.)
2. Is the size right? (Will we be back in 6 months for another?)
3. Is the selection defensible? (Can we explain each cut to an outside party?)
4. Are we treating departing employees with dignity (severance, references, outplacement)?
5. What's the plan for the remaining team in the first 30 days after?

If any answer is weak, slow down and fix it. The cost of a botched layoff is a year of damage; the cost of a week's delay to do it right is trivial.
