# WeWork IPO Failure (2019)

## Situation

WeWork, a flexible office space provider, filed its S-1 prospectus in August 2019 targeting a $47 billion valuation — the valuation SoftBank had assigned in its January 2019 funding round. SoftBank had invested over $10 billion in the company. WeWork had $1.8 billion in revenue in 2018 but lost $1.9 billion, giving it a negative gross margin on a fully-loaded basis. The S-1 introduced the term "space-as-a-service" and "community-adjusted EBITDA," a non-GAAP metric that excluded rent, building, and tenancy costs — the primary costs of the business.

## What Happened

Public market investors and analysts read the S-1 and rejected the valuation. The filing revealed: (1) CEO Adam Neumann had sold $700 million in stock and taken $380 million in loans against WeWork shares before the IPO; (2) Neumann had personally trademarked the word "We" and sold the trademark back to WeWork for $5.9 million; (3) Neumann controlled the company through super-voting shares with 20 votes per share; (4) WeWork had leased buildings it owned personally back to WeWork itself. Investor feedback forced the IPO to be postponed, then cancelled. WeWork's valuation fell to $8 billion by October 2019. SoftBank took control, ousting Neumann with a $1.7 billion exit package. WeWork eventually went public via SPAC in 2021 at a $9 billion valuation and filed for bankruptcy in 2023.

## Root Cause

SoftBank's January 2019 funding round — done at $47 billion — set a valuation that the fundamentals could not support. The company's business model (sign long-term leases, sub-let short-term) was a leveraged real-estate bet, not a technology company. Governance mechanisms that would have constrained founder behavior — an independent board, conflict-of-interest policies, standard executive compensation limits — were either absent or overridden by Neumann's voting control. The S-1 disclosed all of this; the public markets priced it appropriately.

## Key Decision Failures

- **Valuation set by a strategic investor, not the market**: SoftBank's $47B round was a negotiated private price, not a market-clearing price; it bore no relationship to what public investors would pay
- **Non-GAAP metrics invented to hide losses**: "Community-adjusted EBITDA" excluded the cost of the business's core operation; the metric had no standard definition and no precedent
- **Founder self-dealing not addressed before IPO**: Related-party transactions (trademark sale, personal building leases, pre-IPO stock sales) were disclosed in the S-1 rather than resolved before filing
- **Governance structure incompatible with public market norms**: 20:1 super-voting shares with no sunset provision gave Neumann permanent control over a company he had financially extracted from
- **Board did not function as independent check**: Board members approved the trademark repurchase and the lease arrangements rather than refusing or demanding remediation

## Lessons

1. **Private valuation is not public valuation**: A price set by a strategic investor with non-financial motives (market position, portfolio signaling) is not evidence of what the public market will pay. Build the public-market valuation model independently.
2. **Governance problems compound at IPO**: Issues that are tolerable in a private company — related-party transactions, dual-class shares, founder loans — become IPO-killing disclosures. Resolve them before filing, not after.
3. **Invented metrics are a red flag, not a feature**: Non-GAAP metrics that exclude the primary costs of a business indicate the real economics cannot stand on their own. Investors will construct their own metrics from the GAAP data.
4. **Founder liquidity before IPO signals misaligned incentives**: When a founder sells hundreds of millions before the IPO, public investors are buying a company the founder is already partially exiting.
