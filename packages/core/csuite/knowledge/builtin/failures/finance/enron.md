# Enron Collapse (2001)

## Situation

Enron was the seventh-largest company in the United States by revenue in 2000, reporting $101 billion in annual revenue. Its stock traded at $90 per share. Enron had transformed from a natural gas pipeline operator into what it called an "asset-light" trading and risk-management company — buying and selling energy, bandwidth, and other commodities. Fortune named it "America's Most Innovative Company" six years in a row.

## What Happened

In October 2001, Enron announced a $618 million third-quarter loss and a $1.2 billion reduction in shareholder equity. Within weeks, the SEC began an investigation. By December 2001, Enron had filed for bankruptcy — then the largest in US history. The stock, which had traded at $90 in August 2000, closed at $0.26. 20,000 employees lost their jobs and most of their retirement savings (held in Enron stock). Several executives were convicted of fraud and conspiracy.

## Root Cause

Enron used two mechanisms to hide losses and inflate revenue. First, mark-to-market accounting — legitimate for short-term trading — was applied to long-term contracts. Revenue was recognized at signing based on projected future profits, with no obligation to adjust if those projections proved wrong. Second, Enron created hundreds of Special Purpose Entities (SPEs) — off-balance-sheet partnerships — that purchased Enron's underperforming assets at inflated prices, keeping losses off the consolidated balance sheet. The CFO personally ran several SPEs and collected fees from both sides.

## Key Decision Failures

- **Mark-to-market applied to multi-year contracts**: Recognizing the full present value of a 20-year energy contract at signing, with no subsequent adjustment for actual performance, made revenue entirely discretionary
- **Off-balance-sheet SPEs to hide losses**: Partnerships guaranteed by Enron stock absorbed losses that should have appeared on Enron's books; the arrangements were circular — Enron was guaranteeing its own off-balance-sheet debt
- **Board waived conflict-of-interest policy**: The board explicitly approved the CFO running the SPEs that did business with Enron — the fundamental governance control was voted away
- **Auditor did not audit**: Arthur Andersen earned $52 million from Enron in 2000 ($25M audit, $27M consulting); the consulting relationship created structural incentive to approve accounting that Andersen knew was aggressive
- **Whistleblowers suppressed**: Sherron Watkins' 2001 memo to Ken Lay warning of accounting irregularities was not acted on; outside counsel hired to review her concerns was also Enron's law firm

## Lessons

1. **Revenue recognition discretion is a fraud risk**: Any accounting method that gives management control over when and how much revenue to recognize will eventually be used to manipulate results. Auditors should stress-test recognition assumptions aggressively.
2. **Off-balance-sheet is on-risk**: If a company is guaranteeing the obligations of an entity, that entity's losses are the company's losses, regardless of whether they appear on the balance sheet.
3. **Board independence requires economic independence**: Directors who approve exceptions to conflict-of-interest policies for executives running related-party transactions are not functioning as independent overseers.
4. **Audit fees create structural conflicts**: An auditor earning more in consulting fees than audit fees from the same client has a structural incentive incompatible with independent audit judgment.
