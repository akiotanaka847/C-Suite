# Kodak and the Digital Camera (2012 Bankruptcy)

## Situation

Eastman Kodak invented the digital camera in 1975. Engineer Steve Sasson built the first prototype — a 3.6 kg device that captured 0.01 megapixel images to a cassette tape. Kodak shelved the project. For the next three decades, Kodak dominated the film photography market, commanding 90% of US film sales and 85% of camera sales at its peak. By 2012, Kodak had filed for bankruptcy.

## What Happened

Kodak's leadership understood digital photography was coming. Multiple internal studies between 1981 and 2001 forecast that digital would eventually displace film. The company made significant digital investments — it launched a digital camera line, acquired an online photo-sharing startup (Ofoto), and developed digital printing technology. But each digital initiative was evaluated against the profitability of film: gross margins on film and paper exceeded 60%; digital cameras and printing competed against lower-margin hardware. Digital was never allowed to cannibalize film, and so Kodak never built a digital business large enough to replace it. Canon, Sony, and Fujifilm did not have film margins to protect. They built digital aggressively. Kodak lost the camera market, then the printing market, then the photo-sharing opportunity (Ofoto was sold for $25 million; Instagram sold for $1 billion the same year Kodak declared bankruptcy).

## Root Cause

Kodak's P&L was optimized for film. Every resource allocation decision — capital, talent, management attention — flowed to the highest-margin business. Digital was incubated inside the core business and held to the same margin standards, which it could not meet at early scale. No separate P&L for digital was created with the mandate and budget to win at lower margins during the transition. The organization's incentives and measurement systems actively suppressed the disruption.

## Key Decision Failures

- **Evaluating the disruption against the incumbent's margins**: Digital photography could never compete against 60%+ film gross margins in early years; the comparison made the disruption look unattractive until it was too late
- **No separate organizational unit with autonomy**: Digital projects lived inside film business units with film-business incentives; no one was accountable for digital winning
- **Protecting the core over seizing the transition**: Kodak slowed digital launches to avoid cannibalizing film revenue in the near term; competitors used that window to build market position
- **Acquiring but not scaling digital assets**: Ofoto was acquired in 2001 but starved of investment; the photo-sharing opportunity was handed to competitors
- **Late recognition that the transition was irreversible**: By the time leadership accepted digital would fully displace film, Kodak had neither the brand position nor the cost structure to compete

## Lessons

1. **The incumbent's margin is the disruptor's opening**: Every market where the incumbent earns high margins is a market where a disruptor can enter at lower margins and still build a viable business. Defend by attacking your own margins first.
2. **Separate P&L for disruptions is not optional**: A new business evaluated against the core business's economics will always lose the internal resource allocation fight. It needs its own mandate, capital, and leadership.
3. **Acquiring a disruption is not the same as building one**: Ofoto was acquired, then neglected. Buying optionality has no value if the option is never exercised.
4. **The window to transition is shorter than it appears from inside**: Incumbents consistently overestimate the time they have to respond. The right moment to cannibalize your own business is before a competitor forces you to.
