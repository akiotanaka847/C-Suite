# New Coke (1985)

## Situation

By the mid-1980s, Pepsi had closed the market share gap with Coca-Cola significantly, driven by the "Pepsi Challenge" — blind taste tests in which more consumers preferred Pepsi's sweeter taste. Coca-Cola's market share had fallen from 60% in the 1940s to 24% by 1983. Internally, Coke reformulated its product to be sweeter, and conducted 200,000 blind taste tests in which New Coke consistently beat both original Coke and Pepsi. On April 23, 1985, Coca-Cola discontinued its original formula and launched New Coke.

## What Happened

The public reaction was immediate and severe. Within 24 hours, Coca-Cola received 8,000 complaint calls — a volume that overwhelmed its phone lines. Within weeks, 400,000 letters had arrived. Protest groups formed. People stockpiled original Coke. A psychiatrist compared the reaction to grief. On July 10, 1985 — 79 days after the launch — Coca-Cola announced it was bringing back the original formula as "Coca-Cola Classic." New Coke was eventually discontinued. The episode became a textbook case in brand management and consumer research limitations.

## Root Cause

Coca-Cola's research tested product preference but not brand attachment. In a blind taste test, consumers are evaluating liquid in a cup. When Coca-Cola announced the change, consumers were not losing a beverage — they were losing something that was bound up with personal history, national identity, and cultural continuity. The research instrument was correct for the question "which liquid tastes better?" and entirely wrong for the question "should we replace Coke?" The distinction — product preference vs. brand relationship — was not tested.

## Key Decision Failures

- **Research instrument mismatched to decision**: Blind taste tests measure gustatory preference, not the value consumers place on the existing brand relationship; the research was valid, the question was wrong
- **No test of the announcement itself**: Coca-Cola did not test consumer reactions to the idea of discontinuing the original formula; the research told them consumers would like New Coke, not that they would accept losing Classic Coke
- **Underestimating brand as cultural artifact**: Coca-Cola had become part of American cultural identity in a way that made changing its formula feel like a violation of something beyond the product — a dimension the research did not measure
- **Discontinuing the original rather than adding a variant**: The decision to remove rather than extend was the irreversible step; adding a sweeter variant would have allowed market validation without the brand risk
- **Competitive framing drove the wrong question**: Pepsi's taste-test success led Coke to frame the problem as "we need to taste better than Pepsi" rather than "we need to understand what makes Coke valuable to its consumers"

## Lessons

1. **Product research and brand research are different instruments**: Consumers can simultaneously prefer a competitor's product in a blind test and be unwilling to accept their preferred brand's formula being changed. Both things are true. Test both.
2. **Subtraction triggers stronger reactions than addition**: Removing something people value generates disproportionate backlash compared to adding something new. When in doubt, extend rather than replace.
3. **Brand equity accumulates in ways that are not captured by preference data**: The value of a brand includes history, habit, cultural association, and identity — none of which appear in a taste test. Market research that ignores these dimensions is incomplete.
4. **The competitive framing can produce the wrong answer**: Reacting to a competitor's success by adopting their strategy cedes the ability to define your own brand's terms of value.
