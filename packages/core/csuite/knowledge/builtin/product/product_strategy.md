# Product Strategy

Product strategy is the set of choices that determine which problems you solve, for whom, in what order, and how you win. It is downstream of company strategy and upstream of the roadmap. Most product failures are strategy failures dressed up as execution problems.

## What Product Strategy Actually Is

Three layers, often confused:

1. **Vision** — the long-term aspiration. The world you're trying to create. Stable over years. Inspirational, not operational.

2. **Strategy** — the choices about HOW you'll move toward the vision. Which customers, which problems, which not. Updated every 12-18 months when the world changes.

3. **Roadmap** — what you're building in the next 1-2 quarters. Outputs in service of the strategy.

A product team without a strategy has a roadmap that reads as a feature list. A product team with a strategy has a roadmap that reads as a coherent argument.

## The Strategy Stack

A well-formed product strategy answers, in order:

**1. Who is the customer?**
- Specific segment, named pain
- Often narrower than the company-level ICP (a product can lead a wedge into a tighter segment)

**2. What problem are you solving for them?**
- The Job To Be Done — what they're trying to accomplish
- The current alternatives (including doing nothing) and where they fall short
- The cost of the problem unsolved (time, money, risk, missed opportunity)

**3. Why you?**
- Your unique insight or capability
- Why competitors can't credibly do this
- Why this customer would choose you over the alternatives

**4. How do you win over time?**
- Where you start (entry point) and where you expand (the wedge → platform path)
- The compounding advantage (data, network, scale, brand)
- What you explicitly do not do

**5. How do you measure success?**
- The North Star metric
- Leading indicators that move 6-12 weeks before the North Star
- The next 1-2 product bets and what success looks like for each

If you can't answer any of these in 1-2 paragraphs each, you don't have a strategy yet.

## Vision — Writing One That Matters

A vision is not a slogan. It's a description of the future state your product enables.

**Tests of a good vision:**
- Specific enough that a team can decide what it means for their work
- Inspirational enough that people choose your company over comparable ones
- Stable for 5-10 years; if it changes every year, it's a tactic not a vision
- Anchored in a real customer problem, not a technology

**Bad vision examples:**
- "Build amazing products that change the world"
- "Be the leader in [our category]"
- "Make our customers successful"

**Good vision examples:**
- (Tesla) "Accelerate the world's transition to sustainable energy"
- (Stripe) "Increase the GDP of the internet"
- (Notion) "Make software toolmaking ubiquitous"

Each is specific, ambitious, and decade-stable.

## The North Star Metric

A single number that represents the value your product delivers and predicts your business success.

**Properties of a good North Star:**
- Customer-value oriented (not revenue-direct)
- Measurable weekly or monthly
- Sensitive enough to move with real product changes
- The whole company can rally around it

**Examples:**
- Airbnb: Nights Booked
- Spotify: Time Spent Listening
- Slack: Daily Active Teams
- HubSpot: Weekly Active Users

**Bad North Star choices:**
- Revenue (lagging, abstract, doesn't tell product what to build)
- Signups (vanity; doesn't measure value delivered)
- Total users (no quality signal)

The North Star should make obvious what is and isn't a product win. If a launch moves the North Star, it was worth doing.

## Input vs. Output Metrics

Output metrics are what you ultimately care about (NSM, revenue, retention). Input metrics are what you can directly influence (activation rate, feature adoption, time-to-first-value).

**The discipline:**
- Set goals on outputs
- Manage by inputs
- Test inputs to find which ones actually drive outputs (correlation isn't causation)

Most failed product strategies have great input metrics that don't move the outputs. The fix is usually finding new inputs that DO move them, not pushing harder on the wrong ones.

## Strategy Patterns

**Wedge → platform**
- Enter with a narrow product for a specific user
- Expand product breadth or audience over time
- Examples: Slack (engineering team chat → enterprise collab), Shopify (storefront → commerce platform)

**Disruption from below**
- Start serving customers the incumbent doesn't want (smaller, lower-end)
- Move upmarket as your product improves
- Examples: classic Christensen pattern (mini-mills, PCs, MOOCs)

**Premium repositioning**
- Start at the high end, slowly expand down
- Examples: Tesla (Roadster → Model S → Model 3)

**Platform / two-sided market**
- Build value for one side, attract the other
- Examples: Airbnb, Uber, App Store

**Best-in-class point solution**
- Win deeply in one job; don't expand prematurely
- Examples: Figma in design, Linear in issue tracking
- Risk: getting bundled out of existence by a platform play

**The bet to make explicit:**
- Which pattern are we playing?
- What does winning look like in 3 years if the pattern holds?
- What signal would tell us the pattern isn't working?

## What Not to Do

Product strategy is as much about subtraction as addition.

**Anti-strategy patterns:**
- "Build it and they will come" — features without distribution
- Roadmap-as-customer-request-list — squeakiest wheel wins; no coherent direction
- Adjacent expansion without core consolidation — leaving early customers behind
- Imitating competitors' moves — you're always one quarter behind, never differentiated
- "All segments are important" — diluting investment across too many customer types

The discipline: every quarter, name the 3 things you decided NOT to do, and the rationale. If you can't, you're not making strategic choices.

## Strategy Validation

How do you know if your strategy is working?

**Leading signals (6-12 weeks):**
- Engagement metrics improving in target segment
- Sales velocity improving for ICP customers (vs. non-ICP)
- Customer language echoing your positioning
- Product complaints shifting toward "you're missing X" (not "this doesn't work")

**Lagging signals (1-2 quarters):**
- Win rate against named competitors
- Net Revenue Retention improving
- Time-to-first-value declining
- ARR growth accelerating

**Strategy failure signals:**
- Win rates not improving despite product improvements
- High CAC and slow payback even on best segments
- Customer success calling out the same gap repeatedly
- Internal teams pulling in different directions

When you see the failure signals, don't double down. Run a strategy review.

## When to Change Strategy

Most strategies need refresh every 12-18 months. Triggers:

- Significant market shift (new platform, new regulation, new entrant)
- You've achieved the current strategy's milestone (won the wedge — now what?)
- Underperformance against leading indicators for 6+ months
- Major customer feedback themes that don't fit the current strategy

**Refresh process:**
- 4-6 weeks
- Customer interviews (won, lost, churned, target)
- Competitive landscape update
- Internal alignment workshop with leadership and product leadership
- Communicated to the company explicitly — not a quiet shift

A strategy refresh communicated as "evolution" rather than "we're changing course" is mostly cosmetic. If something material is changing, name it.

## The Test of Good Product Strategy

Pull three product managers into a room and ask, separately, in 60 seconds:
1. Who is our target customer?
2. What problem do we solve for them?
3. What are we building this quarter that moves us forward?
4. What did we explicitly choose not to build?
5. What would tell us our strategy is wrong?

If their answers don't align, the strategy hasn't shipped to the team. Fix that before judging execution.
