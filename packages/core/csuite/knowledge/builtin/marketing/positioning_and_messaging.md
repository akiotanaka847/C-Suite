# Positioning and Messaging

Positioning is where strategy meets language. It is the act of placing your product in the customer's mind, against alternatives, in a way that makes the choice to buy you feel obvious.

## What Positioning Is (and Is Not)

**Positioning is:**
- The frame of reference the customer uses to evaluate you
- The category you compete in (or invent)
- The customer for whom you're the obvious choice
- The alternative you make look wrong

**Positioning is NOT:**
- Tagline writing
- Brand voice and visual identity
- Mission and vision statements
- Feature lists or product descriptions

You can have great copy and bad positioning. The bad positioning will lose you the deal anyway.

## April Dunford's Positioning Framework

The most operationally useful positioning framework. Five inputs produce positioning:

1. **Competitive alternatives** — what would customers use if you didn't exist? (Including manual processes, doing nothing, internally built solutions)
2. **Unique attributes** — what features or capabilities do you have that the alternatives don't?
3. **Value (benefits)** — what value does each unique attribute deliver to the customer?
4. **Best-fit customer characteristics** — for which customers does that value matter most? (Demographics, firmographics, situations, pains)
5. **Market category** — the frame of reference that triggers the customer's pre-existing understanding and budget

The positioning **statement** then reads:

> For [best-fit customer], in [market category], who has [problem], [product] is the only solution that [unique value], because [unique attributes].

Test: a salesperson should be able to use this to qualify deals. A marketer should be able to use it to write a homepage in 30 minutes.

## Category Design

You can choose to compete in an existing category or define a new one. Both are valid; the choice has very different implications.

**Compete in existing category:**
- *Pros*: customer understands the category, has a budget for it, knows how to evaluate it
- *Cons*: comparison is direct, often against entrenched incumbent; differentiation must be sharp
- *When*: late entrant in a mature market; well-defined buyer; you're better at a specific dimension

**Define a new category:**
- *Pros*: own the conversation, framing of competition is yours, premium pricing if the category resonates
- *Cons*: education burden is enormous, sales cycle is longer, budget doesn't exist yet
- *When*: you have a genuine shift in approach; well-funded enough to teach the market; targeting buyers with budget flexibility

A common mistake is half-committing — claiming a new category in your marketing while selling against an old category's competitors. Customers see through this immediately.

## The Customer's Job-to-be-Done

Customers don't buy products. They hire products to do a job. The right positioning frames your product as the right hire for a specific job.

**Job statement structure:**
> When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome].

Example: "When I'm preparing for next quarter's board meeting [situation], I want to assemble a clear strategic narrative supported by data [motivation], so I can secure board alignment on my plan and avoid surprise pushback [outcome]."

Notice: the job is not "create a deck." That's a task. The job is what the customer is actually trying to accomplish.

**Why JTBD matters for positioning:**
- Reveals the real competitive set (anything else they could hire to do that job)
- Defines the "good enough" alternative you have to beat
- Specifies the emotional and social benefits the product must deliver (not just functional)

## Messaging Hierarchy

Positioning produces messaging — the words you actually use to talk to customers. Build it as a hierarchy:

**Level 1 — One sentence (the positioning statement, simplified)**
- Goes on the homepage hero
- Spoken in the first 10 seconds of every sales call
- Memorable enough that customers can repeat it

**Level 2 — Three pillars (the supporting value propositions)**
- Three benefits that flow from the positioning
- Each pillar gets its own homepage section, sales slide, product page
- Should be parallel in structure ("Faster X, cheaper Y, better Z")

**Level 3 — Proof (evidence each pillar is real)**
- Customer stories, metrics, comparisons, demos, screenshots
- Specific. Generic proof ("trusted by industry leaders") signals lack of real proof
- Customer language preferred over your language

**Level 4 — FAQs (objection handlers)**
- The 10-15 questions buyers actually ask
- Anti-positioning: explicitly state what you don't do, who you're not for. Saves both parties time.

## Common Positioning Failures

**1. Feature soup** — listing features instead of positioning. Customer can't tell why they should care or how you're different.

**2. "Everyone is our customer"** — refusing to narrow the ICP. Outcome: marketing speaks to no one, sales chases bad-fit deals, product roadmap pulled in every direction.

**3. Positioning against the wrong competitor** — you think you compete with Vendor A; customers evaluate you against an Excel spreadsheet. Outcome: you win the feature comparison and lose the deal because the budget went to "manual is fine."

**4. Bottoming out on price** — "we're cheaper" is the easiest position to take and the easiest to lose. Anyone with deeper pockets can underprice you.

**5. Confusing positioning with branding** — branding is how you make customers feel; positioning is how customers compare you to alternatives. Need both, but don't substitute one for the other.

**6. Positioning that the product can't back up** — promising "10x faster" when you're 1.5x faster. Customers churn, refunds happen, reputation suffers.

## When to Reposition

Most companies need to reposition every 18-36 months. Triggers:

- Market category has matured and your differentiation has commoditized
- You've moved upmarket (or down) and the old positioning targets the wrong buyer
- A new competitor has changed the conversation
- Your product has expanded scope materially
- Win rates are declining for reasons you can't explain through funnel data

**Repositioning process (8-12 weeks):**
1. Customer interviews — won deals, lost deals, churned customers, target buyers (4-6 each)
2. Competitive teardown — what's the alternatives' positioning, where are they weak
3. Internal alignment — leadership, sales, product all bought into the new frame
4. Test the new positioning on cold prospects (sales calls, email outreach)
5. Roll out — website, sales materials, product copy, customer comms

A repositioning is more disruptive than a brand refresh. Plan for sales pipeline volatility during the transition.

## The Test of Good Positioning

Pull three salespeople into a room and ask each, separately, in 60 seconds:
1. Who do we sell to?
2. What problem do we solve?
3. What are we the only ones who do?
4. Who do we compete with?
5. Why do we lose?

If their answers don't align, the positioning isn't operational. Fix that before the next campaign.
