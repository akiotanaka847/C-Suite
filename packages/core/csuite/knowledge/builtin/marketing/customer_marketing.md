# Customer Marketing and Advocacy

The customers you already have are typically your highest-leverage marketing asset and the most under-invested area in growth-stage companies. Customer marketing is the discipline of turning happy customers into a compounding distribution engine.

## Why Customer Marketing Matters

The economics are uncomfortable:

- A reference customer can close a deal at 2-3x the rate of cold outreach
- Word-of-mouth is the #1 source of trusted information in B2B buying (Edelman, repeatedly)
- Expansion within existing customers typically has CAC of 10-30% of net new (much lower)
- Top-decile companies see 40%+ of new pipeline from referrals and customer-driven sources

Despite this, most growth-stage companies spend 80%+ of marketing budget on net-new acquisition and < 10% on customer marketing. The imbalance is rarely justified.

## The Customer Advocacy Ladder

Move customers up the ladder deliberately.

1. **Active user** — uses the product
2. **Satisfied customer** — would speak well of you if asked
3. **Promoter** — actively recommends without prompting
4. **Reference** — willing to talk to prospects
5. **Co-marketer** — appears in case studies, panels, joint webinars
6. **Champion** — advocates externally; creates user community

**Movement up the ladder is not automatic.** It happens because:
- You ask
- You provide a structured opportunity (case study, conference panel, customer council)
- You make the experience valuable for them (visibility, network, status)
- You reciprocate (intros, advice, customer success investment)

**The discipline:** for your top 50 customers, know which rung each is on, and what would move them up.

## Net Promoter Score — Use Carefully

NPS is the most common customer-loyalty metric. It's also widely misused.

**The mechanic:**
- "On a scale of 0-10, how likely are you to recommend [company] to a friend or colleague?"
- 9-10: Promoters
- 7-8: Passives
- 0-6: Detractors
- NPS = % Promoters - % Detractors

**What it's good for:**
- Trend over time (rising = customer experience improving)
- Segmentation (find which segments love you, which struggle)
- Identifying promoters to invite into advocacy programs
- Identifying detractors to intervene before churn

**What it's NOT good for:**
- Absolute benchmarking across industries (very noisy)
- Linking to revenue causally (correlation, yes; causation, complicated)
- Optimizing as the primary metric (Goodhart's Law applies)

**The follow-up question matters more than the number:**
- After the rating: "What's the primary reason for your score?"
- Tag the answers; trend the themes
- The "why" is what you act on

## Reference Programs

Building and managing a roster of reference customers.

**Tiered reference structure:**

- **Speakers**: top reference customers willing to speak at events, on panels, in webinars (10-25 customers typically)
- **References**: willing to take 1-2 calls per quarter from prospects (50-100+ customers)
- **Quoted references**: willing to provide quotes for the website, case studies (200-500+ customers)
- **Logos**: willing to be named on the customer-logo page (most paying customers, usually)

**Reference operations:**
- CRM-tracked who's on which tier, what topics they'll speak to, recent engagement
- Reference rotation: don't burn out the top references; rotate through the list
- "Reference fatigue" is real — limit any one reference to 4-6 calls per year
- Compensate references with: access to product roadmap, executive time, advisory board invitations, conference speaking slots

**Reference call discipline:**
- Brief the reference before the call (who the prospect is, what they'll likely ask)
- Don't coach answers — references are trusted because they're honest
- Debrief after (what came up, any product feedback)
- Thank publicly when appropriate

## Case Studies

Long-form customer stories with named accounts, specific outcomes, and quotable details.

**Anatomy of a great case study:**
1. **The customer's situation** — industry, size, what they were trying to do
2. **The challenge** — specific problem with measurable cost
3. **Why they chose you** — alternatives considered, decision criteria
4. **What they implemented** — specifics, not just "they deployed [product]"
5. **The results** — quantified outcomes, ideally before/after
6. **Quotes** — from a named executive, in their own voice
7. **Look-ahead** — what's next for them with you

**Production discipline:**
- Customer must approve the published version
- Quotes pulled from real interview, not written by marketing
- Permission to use logo, name, and quotes documented
- 2-4 quarters of refresh cadence (case studies get stale)

**Distribution:**
- Featured on website (organized by industry, use case, or company size)
- Linked from sales materials and outbound sequences
- Excerpted in social and emails
- Shared with the customer for their use (employee newsletter, etc.) — they often promote on your behalf

**Aim for:**
- 1-2 new case studies per quarter
- Total of 10-30 active case studies across segments
- Refresh oldest 1-2 per year

## Customer Advisory Boards

A small group of customer executives who provide strategic guidance.

**Composition:**
- 8-15 customers
- Mix of: largest accounts, fast-growers, vocal advocates, segment representatives
- Senior decision-makers (VPs and C-suite)
- Diversity across industries, geographies, use cases

**Cadence:**
- Quarterly meetings (often 1 in-person per year, others virtual)
- Roadmap previews
- Strategic feedback on positioning, product, market direction
- Peer networking (members value the network as much as the company input)

**What CABs are good for:**
- Strategic validation (testing major product or positioning bets)
- Deep relationship building with top accounts
- Identifying patterns across enterprise customers
- Generating co-marketing opportunities

**What CABs are NOT good for:**
- Tactical product feedback (use customer interviews + research)
- Lead generation
- Pure marketing event (members can detect this; trust evaporates)

**The trap:** treating CAB members as ATMs for asks. They're peers; reciprocity matters. Give before you ask.

## User Communities

Customer-to-customer interaction owned (or facilitated) by you.

**Forms:**
- Slack / Discord community (low overhead; active product orgs)
- Public forum / discourse (higher overhead; better SEO)
- In-person events (highest investment; strongest bonds)
- LinkedIn group (cheap; lower engagement)

**Why communities matter:**
- Customers help each other — reduces support load
- Power users surface advanced use cases
- Community becomes a moat (network effects within the customer base)
- Recruiting pipeline (community members become hires)

**Common community failures:**
- Built and abandoned (no community manager investment)
- Over-managed (every post moderated; feels corporate)
- No clear value proposition for joining (why should I be here?)
- No clear value proposition for participating (why should I post?)

**Community investment:**
- Dedicated community manager (full-time at >500 active members)
- Programming (regular events, AMAs, content)
- Recognition (badges, levels, customer-of-the-month features)
- Connection back to product team (escalating community insights, responding visibly)

## Customer Events

In-person events for customers.

**User conference:**
- Annual flagship event (Dreamforce, INBOUND, KubeCon)
- High investment ($500k - $5M+); high impact when done right
- Reserved for companies with significant customer base and ecosystem

**Regional dinners:**
- 8-15 customers in a city, executive hosted
- Modest investment ($5-15k per event)
- Excellent for customer expansion and relationship deepening
- Often best ROI customer marketing investment for growth-stage companies

**Webinars:**
- Lower cost, broader reach, weaker engagement
- Best when customer-led (your customer hosting peers)
- Recorded for evergreen distribution

**Hackathons / training:**
- Skill-building for customers
- Builds product depth and advocacy
- Often paired with certification programs

## Customer Advocacy Programs (Formal)

A program with structured incentives for customer participation.

**Common program elements:**
- Points or status for completing actions (writing a review, providing a reference, speaking at an event)
- Rewards (gift cards, charitable donations, product perks, recognition)
- Status tiers (e.g., "Ambassador", "Champion")
- Exclusive benefits at higher tiers (early access, executive engagement, conference passes)

**Examples:**
- Salesforce Trailblazer
- HubSpot Solutions Partners
- Notion Champions

**When to build a formal program:**
- 500+ active customers
- Track record of customer requests for engagement
- Resources to maintain (typically 1-2 FTEs)

**When NOT to:**
- Pre-PMF
- Small customer base where 1:1 relationships work
- No customer marketer to run it

## Online Reviews (G2, Capterra, TrustRadius)

Reviews on third-party sites have become primary in B2B research.

**Why they matter:**
- 70%+ of B2B buyers consult review sites during evaluation
- Search engines surface review sites for category terms
- Filter and comparison features influence shortlist decisions

**Driving reviews:**
- Ask satisfied customers (post-renewal, post-positive-support-experience)
- Automate the ask (email sequence, in-product prompt)
- Don't game ratings (review sites detect and penalize)
- Respond to negative reviews substantively (others read them)

**Review-site marketing:**
- "Leader in [category]" badges (carry weight in buying processes)
- Sponsored placements (carry less weight; mostly visibility plays)
- Comparison content (some review sites publish vs.-pages that prospects find)

## Common Customer Marketing Failures

1. **No dedicated owner** — customer marketing is "everyone's job" = no one's job
2. **Asking without giving** — burning out customers with requests without reciprocity
3. **Underinvesting in references at scale** — high-touch with the top 5, ignoring the next 50
4. **Generic case studies** — formulaic, undifferentiated, without specific outcomes
5. **CAB as marketing event** — losing trust by treating advisors as buyers
6. **Treating customer marketing as "free marketing"** — it isn't; it requires investment to compound

## The Customer Marketing Diagnostic

For your current state, ask:
1. What % of new pipeline came from customer-driven sources last quarter?
2. How many customers are on our reference list? When did we last refresh it?
3. What's the cadence of case study production? Are they distributed?
4. Do we have a customer marketer? Or is it split across roles?
5. When was the last time a customer told us they enjoy being a reference?

If the answer to #5 is "never asked", you have a customer marketing program that takes more than it gives. Reverse that, and the advocacy compounds.
