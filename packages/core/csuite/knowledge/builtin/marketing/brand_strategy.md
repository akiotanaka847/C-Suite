# Brand Strategy

A brand is not your logo. A brand is the predictable feeling customers have when they encounter you. Done well, brand compounds: it earns premium pricing, accelerates sales, attracts talent, and builds defensibility no competitor can match. Done badly, brand is a $200k logo refresh and a deck nobody reads.

## What Brand Actually Is (Operationally)

A brand has three components:

1. **Identity** — what we say we are (mission, values, voice, visual design)
2. **Perception** — what customers think we are (their actual associations)
3. **Reality** — how we actually behave (what we ship, who we hire, how we handle hard moments)

The gap between these three is where brand problems live. A brand strategy is the work of aligning them.

**The cheap brand failure:** investing only in identity (new logo, new website) while reality and perception stay the same. Six months later: same brand, more expensive.

**The expensive brand failure:** behaving inconsistently with stated values (we say "transparency" while withholding pricing). Customers and employees lose trust; the brand erodes faster than it can be repaired.

## Why Brand Matters in B2B (Where It's Often Dismissed)

The common B2B argument: "Buyers are rational; brand is for consumer companies."

The data disagrees:
- B2B buyers consider 3-5 vendors on average — being on the shortlist is a brand outcome
- 70%+ of the buying journey happens before vendor contact (Gartner, repeatedly) — brand drives consideration when you're not in the room
- Branded search volume predicts pipeline 6-12 months out
- Strong brands command 10-30% pricing premiums in same product categories

**The two B2B brand outcomes:**
1. *Recognition* — being known by buyers in the category
2. *Preference* — being chosen when shortlisted, often even at higher price

You need both. Recognition without preference produces awareness but lost deals. Preference without recognition means you only win deals among the customers who already know you.

## Brand Architecture

How brands relate to products and sub-brands. Three common patterns:

**Branded House (monolithic):**
- One master brand applied to all products: Google Search, Google Maps, Google Cloud
- Pros: brand investment compounds; consistent customer experience; clear org structure
- Cons: brand failure in one area damages all areas; products with very different audiences can't differentiate

**House of Brands:**
- Multiple distinct brands under a parent: Procter & Gamble owns Tide, Pampers, Gillette
- Pros: each brand can target distinct segments; failure of one doesn't damage others
- Cons: high marketing cost (every brand needs its own investment); fragmented marketing org

**Endorsed Brands (hybrid):**
- Sub-brand with parent endorsement: Marriott Courtyard, Marriott Residence Inn
- Pros: leverages parent equity while allowing sub-brand differentiation
- Cons: complexity; sub-brands can be confused with each other

**Choosing architecture:**
- Branded house default for tech companies with related product lines
- House of brands appropriate when target segments / value propositions are very different
- Hybrid for acquired companies retaining recognition while gaining parent endorsement

Don't over-engineer architecture early. Most startups under $50M ARR should be branded house — invest everything in one brand.

## Brand Positioning

Brand positioning is the customer's mental shorthand for who you are and why they should care.

**Strong brand positions:**
- *Volvo*: safety
- *Tesla*: electric vehicles that don't compromise on performance
- *Stripe*: payments built by developers, for developers
- *Notion*: the all-in-one workspace that bends to your team
- *Slack*: where work happens (specifically: chat-first work)

Each one is short, specific, and credibly different from competitors.

**The discipline:**
- Own one word or one concept in the customer's mind
- Reinforce it relentlessly across every customer touchpoint
- Resist the urge to dilute (expanding to mean "more")

## Voice and Tone

How your brand sounds in language.

**Voice** is durable — the personality. Stripe's voice is precise, developer-respectful, calm.

**Tone** flexes with context — same personality, different mode. Stripe's tone in a customer outage announcement is more direct and apologetic than its tone in a developer documentation page.

**Voice attributes (pick 3-4):**
- Confident vs. humble
- Playful vs. serious
- Technical vs. accessible
- Direct vs. diplomatic
- Warm vs. clinical

**Documenting voice:**
- "We say X, we don't say Y" examples
- Sample copy at multiple lengths (tweet, email, landing page section)
- Specific words to use; words to avoid
- Reference material for new writers

The voice document is operational only when it's used as a check on actual copy before publishing.

## Visual Identity

Logo, typography, color palette, imagery, layout system.

**The core asset set:**
- Logo (primary, secondary, monogram, on-light, on-dark)
- Typography (primary font, secondary font, web-safe alternatives)
- Color palette (primary, secondary, accent, semantic colors for status)
- Iconography style
- Photography or illustration style
- Layout principles (grid, spacing, hierarchy)

**The cost trap:**
- $50k-$500k for a "rebrand" with a top agency
- Often: a different logo and color palette; no operational change
- The expensive output without operational discipline produces no compounded value

**Better investment:**
- Distinctive but appropriate visual identity (doesn't need to be Pentagram)
- Strong design system documentation
- Componentized assets (Figma library, brand portal)
- Templates that anyone can use to produce on-brand output
- Periodic audits of how the brand actually appears in the wild

The discipline of consistent application matters more than the originality of the design.

## Brand Building Channels

Where brand actually gets built (and where it doesn't).

**Where brand IS built:**
- Customer experience — the product, the support, the onboarding
- Employee experience — culture, hiring, how employees talk about the company
- Content and thought leadership — founder essays, podcasts, original research
- Long-form storytelling — case studies that make customers heroes
- Distinctive design and language in everything customer-facing
- Repeated, consistent appearance over time

**Where brand IS NOT built (despite common claims):**
- Press releases
- Awards and badges (people don't care)
- Generic blog posts about industry trends
- Social media volume disconnected from substance
- Most "thought leadership" written by content agencies
- Logo refreshes without operational change

## Earned Media and PR

Real PR is a brand-building tool. Most "PR" is wasted spend.

**Effective PR:**
- Founder thought leadership in publications that matter to your buyer
- Customer-driven stories (their success, you're a footnote)
- Original research that becomes the industry's reference point
- Reactive commentary on news events where you have genuine credibility
- Long-form interviews / podcasts where the founder's thinking surfaces

**Ineffective PR:**
- Press releases about product launches (nobody reads them anymore)
- Awards bought via sponsorships
- Generic "industry expert quotes"
- Coverage in publications your customers don't read

**Internal vs. agency:**
- Early-stage: founder-led, with maybe a freelance PR consultant
- Growth-stage: in-house PR lead, with selective agency support
- Late-stage: in-house team plus enterprise agency for specific moments (IPO, major launches)

## Customer Marketing and Advocacy

Your existing customers are the most powerful brand asset you have. Most companies under-invest here.

**Components:**
- Reference customers (named accounts willing to take calls from prospects)
- Case studies (formal stories with outcomes; ~2-4 per quarter)
- Testimonials and quotes (lighter weight; quotable on the website)
- Logos on the website (signaling)
- User events and communities
- Customer advisory boards (strategic feedback + relationship deepening)
- Awards / certifications customers can earn (status-driven engagement)
- User-generated content amplification

**The customer advocacy ladder:**
1. Active user (uses product)
2. Satisfied customer (would recommend if asked)
3. Promoter (would actively recommend)
4. Reference (will talk to prospects)
5. Co-marketer (will appear in case studies, panels, joint webinars)
6. Champion (creates user community, evangelizes externally)

Move customers up the ladder deliberately. The economics of brand are dominated by what your existing customers say.

## Brand Measurement

Brand is measurable, but it requires long time horizons.

**Leading indicators (weekly/monthly):**
- Branded search volume (Google Trends; SEMrush)
- Direct traffic to website
- Social engagement (mentions, sentiment)
- Email open rates and response quality
- Press / podcast mentions in publications that matter

**Lagging indicators (quarterly):**
- Inbound demo requests / leads (especially from "found you via [referral]")
- Win rates at higher prices than competitors
- Reduced sales cycle length for warm leads
- Employer brand metrics (qualified inbound for roles)

**Brand health survey (annual or semi-annual):**
- Unaided recall: "Name three vendors in [category]"
- Aided recall: "Have you heard of [company]?"
- Brand attributes: "If I say [company], what comes to mind?"
- Consideration: "Would you consider [company] for [need]?"
- Preference: "Of [list], which would you choose?"

Track the same questions over time on the same population. Trend is the signal.

## Common Brand Strategy Failures

1. **Rebranding to fix what's actually a positioning problem** — new logo doesn't solve "customers don't understand what you do"
2. **Brand strategy disconnected from product/business** — brand says "innovative", product is incrementalist; gap kills the brand
3. **Inconsistent execution** — every team interprets the brand differently; no central application discipline
4. **One-shot brand investment** — agency project produces a deck; six months later, nothing changed
5. **Vanity metrics** — measuring social engagement and PR hits while customer perception and consideration are flat
6. **Brand as PR's job** — treating brand as "marketing" while customer experience, hiring, and product behavior contradict it

## The Brand Diagnostic

Annually, ask:
1. What three words come to mind when our target customer thinks of us? (Survey them; don't guess.)
2. Where does our reality match the brand identity? Where does it conflict?
3. What's our branded search volume trend over the last 12 months?
4. Which customers would talk to a prospect on our behalf without hesitation?
5. What did we ship this year that strengthened the brand? What weakened it?

If you can't answer #4 with a list, you have a customer-marketing investment to make. That's where most brand leverage actually comes from.
