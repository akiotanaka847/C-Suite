# Content Marketing

Content marketing is the long-cycle, compounding asset class of marketing. Most content programs fail because companies want quarterly results from a year-long investment, then stop just before the curve bends up.

## What Content Marketing Actually Does

Three distinct jobs, often conflated:

1. **Demand capture** — content that captures buyers already searching for solutions (bottom-of-funnel SEO, comparison content, ROI calculators)
2. **Demand creation** — content that creates awareness of problems and approaches (top-of-funnel thought leadership, original research, ecosystem participation)
3. **Customer enablement** — content that helps customers succeed and expand (documentation, best-practice guides, certifications)

Each requires different content, different success metrics, different cadence. A "content strategy" that doesn't distinguish them produces blurry output.

## The Content Funnel

A standard framing, with content types per stage:

**Top-of-funnel (awareness):**
- Industry research and reports
- Founder essays / thought leadership
- Podcasts (hosting or guesting)
- Newsletters
- Video / YouTube series
- Long-form articles on broad topics

*Goal*: build category authority; capture attention before active buying

**Middle-of-funnel (consideration):**
- Framework articles ("how to think about X")
- How-to guides
- Webinars
- Comparison content ("solutions for X")
- Industry-vertical content

*Goal*: shape buyer thinking; demonstrate expertise; capture leads

**Bottom-of-funnel (decision):**
- Customer case studies
- Product-vs.-product comparisons ("Vendor X vs. Vendor Y")
- ROI calculators
- Buyer's guides
- Implementation guides

*Goal*: convert active buyers; provide ammunition for internal champions

**Each stage matters; most companies over-invest in top-of-funnel and under-invest in bottom.**

## The 80/20 Rule of Content

In any content program: 20% of the content drives 80% of the value.

**The 20% almost always includes:**
- 1-3 definitive bottom-of-funnel pieces on high-intent search terms
- 1-2 pieces of original research that become category-defining references
- 1-2 founder essays that articulate a distinct point of view
- 3-5 customer case studies with specific outcomes

**The 80% (the waste):**
- Weekly newsletter recycling industry headlines
- Generic "5 tips for [topic]" listicles
- Press release blog posts
- Conference recap articles
- Content republishing without distribution investment

**The discipline:**
- Identify the 20% candidates ahead of time
- Invest 80% of the content budget on those 20% of pieces
- Be ruthless about killing the remaining 80% of planned content if they won't move metrics

Most content calendars are designed for production volume, not impact. Invert this.

## SEO Strategy

Organic search is the largest source of content-driven pipeline for most B2B businesses. Done right, it compounds for years.

**The hierarchy of SEO targets:**

1. **Bottom-of-funnel commercial terms** — high intent, lower volume, fastest payback
- "[Category] software"
- "[Product type] for [use case]"
- "Best [category] tools"
- "[Competitor] alternatives"
- "[Competitor] vs. [other competitor]"

2. **Middle-of-funnel problem terms** — moderate intent, higher volume
- "How to [solve specific problem]"
- "[Framework name] guide"
- "What is [concept]"

3. **Top-of-funnel category terms** — lower intent, highest volume
- Broad industry topics
- Adjacent themes

**The sequence:**
- Win bottom-of-funnel first (faster ROI, more direct revenue connection)
- Add middle-of-funnel when bottom is producing
- Top-of-funnel last (longest payback)

Companies often do this backward, starting with broad terms that have huge competition and slow payback.

**Page-level SEO discipline:**
- Match intent precisely (a "buy" intent page should look like a buying page, not a 5000-word essay)
- Title and meta description optimized for click-through, not just keyword
- Schema markup where appropriate (FAQ, breadcrumb, product, organization)
- Internal linking from related content
- External backlinks (high-quality, earned, not bought)

**Common SEO failures:**
- Stuffing keywords without serving intent
- Endless blog posts that compete with each other for the same term
- "AI-generated" content at scale (Google increasingly penalizes)
- Ignoring page experience (Core Web Vitals, mobile)
- No measurement loop (publishing without monitoring ranking and traffic)

## Programmatic SEO

Generating many pages programmatically targeting long-tail search terms.

**When it works:**
- You have structured data that maps to user intents at scale (Zapier integration directory, Yelp restaurant pages, Trulia property pages)
- Each generated page genuinely answers the user's question
- Pages have differentiation, not just templated copy

**When it doesn't:**
- Templated pages with no real value (Google has gotten very good at detecting this)
- "AI-generated content at scale" used as a shortcut
- No data underlying the pages — just keyword-stuffed templates

Programmatic SEO can be a huge unlock for the right business but is widely abused. The bar for what counts as "valuable to users" is rising rapidly.

## Distribution > Creation

A truth that most content programs ignore: distribution is more important than creation.

A great piece nobody sees is worth less than a mediocre piece distributed broadly.

**The distribution mindset:**
- Plan distribution BEFORE creating the content
- For each piece: where will it appear, who will share it, how will it find an audience?
- Budget 50%+ of content effort on distribution, not creation

**Distribution channels:**
- Email (your list — the only channel you fully own)
- LinkedIn (most valuable B2B social channel)
- Twitter / X (declining for B2B but still relevant for tech)
- Podcast guesting (10x the reach of your own content, in many cases)
- Industry community participation (Slack groups, Discord, subreddits)
- Partner co-distribution (newsletter swaps, co-published research)
- Paid amplification (LinkedIn ads on best-performing organic pieces)
- Search (the long-term compound channel)

## Email and Newsletter Strategy

The most underrated channel — and the most owned.

**Newsletter principles:**
- Single voice (specific writer, not "the team")
- Specific point of view (not just curated links)
- Consistent cadence (weekly or biweekly best for most B2B)
- Length matching the audience attention (often 500-1500 words)
- Personal subject lines (not "Newsletter #47")

**The newsletter as a brand asset:**
- Best newsletters become a primary brand expression (Stratechery, Lenny's, Pirate Wires)
- A founder-written newsletter is one of the highest-leverage brand investments available
- Cadence matters more than perfection — 50 mediocre issues beat 5 perfect ones

**Owned audience is the moat:**
- Algorithms change; platforms decline (Twitter post-2022, Facebook organic reach over 15 years)
- Email lists are durable, transferable, monetizable
- Build your owned audience deliberately even if other channels are working

## Founder-Led Content

In early-stage and growth companies, the founder's voice is often the highest-leverage content asset.

**Why founder content works:**
- Authentic in a market full of corporate voice
- Unique POV that the company embodies
- Drives recruiting, fundraising, sales pipeline simultaneously
- Doesn't require corporate review committee (faster, sharper)

**Founder content discipline:**
- 1-2 hours per week consistently beats sporadic bursts
- Long-form essays > short social posts (compound longer)
- Podcasts as the second-best founder content channel (low marginal effort, high reach)
- Find the format that fits the founder's communication style (writers vs. talkers vs. doers/demonstrators)

**Pitfalls:**
- Hot-takes that age badly
- Topics drift away from what the company does
- Time investment crowds out actual CEO work

## Measuring Content

The right metrics depend on the job.

**For demand capture (bottom-of-funnel SEO):**
- Organic traffic to commercial pages
- Conversion rate from traffic to lead
- Pipeline attributed to organic source
- Time to payback (months from publication to attributed revenue)

**For demand creation (top-of-funnel):**
- Branded search volume trend (lagging indicator of brand strength)
- Subscriber growth (newsletter, podcast, YouTube)
- Time on page / scroll depth (engagement signals)
- Press citations and quotations (third-party validation)

**For customer enablement:**
- Documentation page views (especially deep-search)
- Time-to-first-value improvements (correlated with onboarding content)
- Support ticket deflection rates
- Certification completion (where applicable)

**Don't measure:**
- "Engagement" without conversion (Likes alone are vanity)
- Total content output (volume isn't the goal)
- "Reach" without recall (a one-time view isn't brand)

## Content Operations

Real content programs are run as operations, not as creative endeavors.

**Roles:**
- Content strategist (decides what to produce and why)
- Writers (often a mix of in-house and contractors)
- Editor (ensures quality, voice consistency, accuracy)
- SEO specialist (informs strategy with search data, optimizes pages)
- Distribution lead (owns getting it seen)
- Designer (essential for any visual or formatted content)

**Operational cadence:**
- Quarterly: editorial calendar planning, theme review
- Monthly: performance review, content priorities refresh
- Weekly: in-flight content review, distribution planning
- Daily: writing, editing, publishing

**Tools (without endorsing specifics):**
- CMS (content publishing)
- Editorial calendar tool
- SEO platform (Ahrefs, SEMrush, etc.)
- Email service provider (substantial market)
- Analytics (GA, plus content-specific tools)

## The Content Diagnostic

For your content program, ask:
1. What 3 pieces produced last year drove the most measurable pipeline?
2. What % of content production effort went into the top 3?
3. What was our investment in distribution vs. creation?
4. Which channels are growing? Which are declining? What's next?
5. If we cut content output by 50% but kept distribution constant, would results change much?

If #5 is "probably not", the content program is over-producing. The leverage isn't more content — it's better content, distributed better.
